THE PITCH

Something is moving through the blood of New York's Kindred.

It began as rumour — a fever in the thin-bloods, a bad taste in the vitae south of Canal Street. Now the Rack is unsafe, the subways have gone strange, and a cult of the discarded is chanting something in the dark under the city that is not any language a human throat was built for.

The Court is coming apart. Elders who have kept their peace for a century are accusing one another of treachery, and the Prince is in a condition that the Sheriff will not describe in Elysium. Whatever is in the blood, it has climbed high enough to be political.

The coterie was there when it happened: a nightclub, two in the morning, the music stopping. Afterwards the Sheriff hands them the investigation. It is not a prestigious assignment. They are young enough to still be clean, and expendable enough to disown.

The thread they pull leads out of the city, two hundred miles north, to a town the Camarilla has not thought about since 1894 — where the people smile at nothing, there is a lodge at the foot of the ridge that nobody can find, and something has been lying under the black stone since the pyramids were new.

⚠ FOR THE STORYTELLER ONLY

Everything in this section is what the players will spend the entire chronicle assembling. None of it is ever stated to them outright. The final pieces do not land until Chapter Six.

I. The Sleep — c. 2650 BC

Sekhemkhet, Pharaoh of the Third Dynasty and an Antediluvian, grew weary of the world. He did not want to die. He wanted to set the world down, and take it up again when it had become worth holding.

He commissioned his childe — his High Priest, his architect, the first vampire he ever made — to build him a tomb and seal him into a peaceful torpor.

On the Architect's name. He was called Kanefer. No one has spoken it aloud in four thousand years, and the Storyteller should not either. He is the Architect in his own mouth, the Bound Architect in Ashcroft's later papers, the Unsleeping Shadow in what survives of the Mohican inheritance, and Our Lord to the Wakeners — who believe they are serving the thing in the tomb, and are in fact serving the thing that answers to it.

On Imhotep. Players who look Sekhemkhet up will find Imhotep's name in graffiti on the enclosure wall of his pyramid, and will conclude the Architect is Imhotep. Let them. Imhotep was the King's mortal master of works, the name history kept. Kanefer was the high priest who carried the plans in his head, and history kept nothing of him at all.

The name surfaces exactly once in the entire chronicle: at the sarcophagus, offered privately to the marked character as part of what he is selling. Not as a revelation. As an intimacy. Here is what I was called. No one has said it in four thousand years.

Until then, a creature whose name has gone out of the world entirely is more frightening than any set of syllables. Sekhemkhet has a name, cut into stone, because he was a king. His childe does not, because he was a servant — and that difference is the whole of their relationship.

The rite required two sleepers. The seal was made from the blood-bond between sire and childe, and a bond cannot be closed from one end. For the King to sleep, the Architect had to sleep beside him.

This was not presented as a price. It was simply how the working was built, and the King never thought to discuss it, because to him it was obvious.

The King went down into the stone at Saqqara believing his childe was following him.

II. The Roaming

The Architect did not follow.

He was young. He was drunk on his own power. The world had just been handed to him with no one left standing above him in it. He was not afraid of the tomb — he simply did not want to stop.

For centuries he roamed. He saw everything the ancient world had to offer and he loved every night of it.

He made childer as he went — carelessly, across half the ancient world. Every Kindred of the King's line alive tonight descends from them, because the King himself made only one.

And every night, his King's rage arrived in his mind.

Not words. The raw fact of an Antediluvian lying awake inside a half-finished working, unable to sleep because the seal had never closed — and knowing precisely whose fault that was.

It did not lessen. Distance means nothing to the bond. Decades became centuries of a fury that does not tire, does not sleep, and does not forgive.

In the end he could not bear it. But he would not return to Egypt to lie down at his sire's side — from fear of what waited there, from pride, and because going back would have been an admission.

III. The Replica — Broken Arrow Ridge

He went as far as land goes: across Asia, across the ice, down into the eastern woodlands of the continent that would one day be called America.

And then he built the tomb again.

He enthralled the people living in the valley, and had them quarry black granite and chisel out the caverns beneath the ridge. He raised a replica of his King's tomb under a pine-covered hill in a place no one had ever heard of.

He performed the missing rites upon himself, drained his workers into the ritual basins, sealed the chamber from the inside, and lay down.

It worked.

The bond closed. Half a world apart, sire and childe fell at last into deep, dreamless, peaceful torpor — together.

Above him, the survivors kept an oath that would outlive its own reason by four thousand years: do not dig on the hill. Settlers displaced them in the 1700s. In 1758 the oath showed its teeth one final time, at the Battle of Broken Arrow Ridge. After that, there were no guardians left.

III½. The Ensign on the Ridge — 14 October 1758

Lysander Ashcroft was there.

He was a young officer in Major Ferguson's company, one of the eighty men who marched onto Broken Arrow Ridge to make camp and raise a small stockade over a flat clearing of black stone. He was present when the pickaxes struck the basalt, and present when the fog came down, and present for the twenty silent minutes that followed.

He died on that hill with the rest of them. Or close enough to it.

Something found him bleeding out on the black stone in the dark and Embraced him there, on top of the tomb, for reasons that had nothing whatever to do with what lay beneath it. A passing Ventrue, an opportunity, a dying officer of good breeding. Coincidence, and nothing more.

When relief scouts arrived three days later, they counted seventy-nine bodies laid out in a precise ring around the exposed stone — from the treeline, because the warriors were still in the woods and nobody would go closer. They assumed the eightieth had been carried off by the attackers. Nobody thought to check whether he had got up.

The bodies were never recovered. When the Crown came back in strength, the ring was gone, and nobody has found the company since. Where seventy-nine men went is a question the records do not answer and the chronicle never needs to.

The records call the attack inexplicable. The local Mohicans were allied to the Crown; nothing in the politics of 1758 explains why they would fall on a British company. Only the oath does.

Everything Ashcroft did for the next hundred and thirty-five years proceeds from that night. His scholarship is not curiosity. It is a man going back over the worst hours of his mortal life, again and again, trying to understand why the warriors came down the hill the way they did, and what the silence meant, and what was underneath him while he bled.

The Court believed he was mad to trade Manhattan for a worthless hamlet of three hundred mortals.

He was going home.

Timeline check: born c. 1733, Embraced October 1758, a hundred and thirty-five years as Kindred before the 1893 purchase. A well-established Ventrue ancilla by the time he petitions the Prince — which is precisely why he wins.

The Architect does not know any of this. He lay in dreamless torpor through 1758; the defence of the ridge was driven by the oath in the Mohican bloodline, not by him. The company never got further than the capstone — the black stone is the roof of the tomb, not its door, and nothing that happens on the roof reaches the sleeper. When a hand finally touched his sarcophagus in 1905, he took the mind of a stranger, and never learned that he was holding one of the men who had been there.

This irony belongs to the players alone. Hold the reveal until Chapter Four.

IV. The Breaking-In — 1893–1905

Lysander Ashcroft dug through the root cellar floor of Greywick Lodge in 1893 and found black basalt.

The same stone he had bled onto, as a mortal, a hundred and thirty-five years before.

It was not a floor. It was a roof. Beneath it lay a tomb the size of a village: passages that turn back on themselves, false chambers, sealed doors cut with a script no one in the western hemisphere could read.

He spent twelve years in the maze. Mapping it, translating its walls, measuring every room against everything he knew of Saqqara, and working out — one sealed door at a time — the rites that had closed it. He was lucid for all of it. He wrote everything down.

In 1905 he reached the burial chamber and put his hand on the sarcophagus.

The first mind to touch it in four thousand years. He was overwritten on contact.

The intrusion cracked the seal partway open. The Architect surfaced into a half-state — aware, but pinned. The sleeping ritual will not release him, because if he rises, his King rises with him. The bond that let them sleep together will not let one of them wake alone.

What he can do: speak into a mind that touches the sarcophagus; hold minds he has already taken; keep every mortal in the valley docile, by an old priestly blood magic that costs him nothing; hide the door to his tomb from every mind he does not own; see the world through borrowed eyes.

What he cannot do: move; reach beyond the valley, except through Ashcroft; touch any mind he does not already own.

For forty years he watched the modern world through Ashcroft's eyes. Motor cars, electric light, the Great War, radio, the second war. And he wanted it — all of it — with a hunger sharpened by four thousand years of having had the world and lost it.

He also saw what had become of his King's blood: thinned across a hundred generations, scattered, weak, and everywhere. Nothing in the modern night could stand against what he had been — and every drop of that blood, however thin, is the one thing the rite recognises.

V. The Plan — The Gathering

He intends to break the sleeping ritual and rise.

The seal was closed with blood, and only blood of the same line can break it: his sire's own. Not a drop, and not from one throat. A great deal of it, old and strong, spilled together at the entrance of the tomb.

The Chant of the Broken Crown — phonemes that resonate in anyone carrying Sekhemkhet's blood. Hear it long enough and you are bound to the Architect's will. It moves through subway tunnels, a few small New York radio stations, cassette tape, and a cult of the disposable. And it feeds on itself: every will it binds strengthens the blood-working woven into it, so each new victim is taken faster than the last. For fifty years it crept. This summer it is climbing a curve. The chronicle begins at the crescendo.

The Binding makes the King's line into ammunition. Thin-bloods and Caitiff are taken in days, and their blood is worth almost nothing. What the Architect needs are elders — centuries of potent vitae — and elders are taken slowest. The Primogen of New York are the harvest, and the harvest is nearly ripe.

The Wakeners — Caitiff, thin-bloods and ghouls, told they are the chosen vanguard of a sleeping god. They are useful idiots: Ashcroft's extension, the Chant's carriers, and at the end, the drivers. Ashcroft is his hands. The cult is his voice. The broadcast is his reach.

The Gathering. When enough powerful Kindred of the line are bound, they will be called north — driven two hundred miles by ghouls in rented vans, lying in the back through the daylight hours — and brought to the entrance of the tomb: the threshold he sealed from inside, where the basins that took his workers' blood still stand. There they will open their own veins together.

Ashcroft only set the seal ajar. The Gathering will shatter it.

It is a huge undertaking, and it is not quick. Moving a dozen ancient, sleepwalking Kindred two hundred miles through daylight takes vans, drivers, safe rooms, and weeks of organisation, and the main body of the Gathering is still being assembled when the coterie reaches Broken Arrow. Stragglers arrive first — a few at a time, early and alone — and they are dangerous enough to make everyone understand what is coming.

He lay down in blood. He will get up the same way. He built the same room twice, and he will perform the same rite twice.

And when he rises, so does his King.

The Architect knows this. He has accepted it. Four thousand years of torpor did not make him weary of the world — it made him starving for it, and he would rather rule a diminished world at the side of a monster than sleep another four thousand years.

The Gathering is the only way he wakes, and it needs nothing from the coterie. Not the marked character, not the players, not even Ashcroft. It is already in motion when the story begins.

If the seal closes, it all stops at once. The Chant is his blood-working, and it dies with his waking. The Dread ends mid-verse. The Binding breaks. Most of the bound snap back into their own minds — in the back of a van on the Thruway, or standing on a silent street in a small town they have never heard of — with no idea why they are there.

VI. What the Coterie Never Suspects

One of the player characters carries Sekhemkhet's blood.

They are not chosen, marked for greatness, or important. The Architect did not send for them and has no plan for them. They are simply of the line, and the Chant is doing to them what it is doing to every other Kindred of the line in New York — only it has not finished yet, because not enough time has passed. They reach the tomb unbound because they are early.

That makes them the most important person in the chamber, from both directions.

Buried in Ashcroft's research — a footnote at the bottom of a pile — is the rite to reseal the tomb — and resealing needs the blood of the King's line. The coterie needs their friend to bleed.

And the Architect, who cannot command a will he does not own, needs that same friend to do nothing. At the sarcophagus, at great effort, he speaks directly into their mind, and no one else hears a word of it.

He will make an offer. It will be honest, and he believes he can deliver on it. He will also give them his name, which he has given to no one since the Third Dynasty — and that, not the offer, is the part designed to land.

Whatever the character decides, the rest of the coterie sees only this: their friend with a hand on the sarcophagus, the rite half-finished around them, and the first of the Gathering already walking up the hill. They cannot tell refusal from consideration, or a mistake from sabotage. What is said there is never heard by anyone else, and the character is under no obligation to report a word of it.

THE SPINE

A creature who could not bear to stop living is about to wake the thing that owns him, because after four thousand years of sleep he would rather have the world on any terms at all.

And he will explain this to the coterie, calmly and reasonably, and he will be wrong about nothing except what he believes he is owed.

PART ONE — THE STORYTELLER'S APPARATUS

1.1 — THE ARCHITECT

Kanefer. The Bound Architect. The Unsleeping Shadow. Our Lord. First childe of Sekhemkhet. High Priest. Master of works. Fourth generation.

What He Is

A vampire of the fourth generation who has been asleep since before the invention of writing in most of the world, and awake — partly, badly, insufficiently — since 1905.

He is not a monster in the sense the coterie will expect. He does not rave. He is not driven by hunger or by the Beast; four thousand years of torpor have left his Beast quieter than a neonate's. He is lucid, courteous, and enormously patient, and the thing that makes him frightening is that everything he says is true.

He built a tomb for a king, refused to lie down in it, and spent centuries paying for that refusal with his sanity intact and no relief available. Then he lay down anyway, and slept for four thousand years, and something dug him halfway out. He is now stuck at the surface of his own sleep, and he wants very badly to come the rest of the way up.

He is aware that doing so wakes Sekhemkhet. He has made his peace with that.

The Half-State

This is the single most important constraint in the chronicle, and the Storyteller should have it fixed before running any scene that touches him.

He is not awake. He is not in torpor either. Ashcroft's hand on the sarcophagus in 1905 cracked the seal partway and left him wedged in between — conscious, aware, and unable to move or rise. The sleeping ritual will not release him, because the seal was closed by two and cannot be opened by one.

Four thousand years of dreamless rest did not restore him. It preserved him. What surfaced in 1905 is what went under: the same mind, the same appetites, the same brilliance, all of it intact and none of it exercised since the Third Dynasty.

What he can do

Speak into a mind that touches the sarcophagus Physical contact with the sarcophagus in the burial chamber, and nothing else — not the basalt roof, not the maze. This is how he took Ashcroft. Reaching a mind he does not own this way costs him enormously; he can hold it open for one conversation, and it is how he will speak to the marked character.
Hold what he has already taken Ashcroft is his absolutely and permanently. Any mind bound through the Chant carries his preference, at any distance, indefinitely — but he does not see through them, hear through them, or steer them.
The ward The Lodge at the foot of the ridge, and the path that leads to it, cannot be seen. Walk toward it and space quietly curves around it: you pass it without knowing, and something feels badly wrong that you cannot name. It is reflex — a sleeper's flinch, costing him nothing — and it has existed only since 1905. It hides the door from minds he does not own; his own see it plainly. (Full detail in §1.7 — The Ward.)
Pacify mortals An ancient priestly blood magic, the way mortals were kept docile in his own day, and entirely separate from the Chant. His capacity for it is effectively endless and costs him virtually nothing. It is why three hundred people in Broken Arrow smile at nothing. Ashcroft's studies contain a section on it that the players can find.
Wake for thirty seconds If anyone attacks the sarcophagus, he spends a portion of his strength on half a minute of true waking. Whoever attacked it is destroyed. It costs him, and he will not do it lightly — but he will do it.
See through borrowed eyes Ashcroft's, and only Ashcroft's — wherever Ashcroft goes, in the valley or two hundred miles away in a Manhattan tunnel. Most of the twentieth century arrived through them.
Wait Better than anything else alive.

What he cannot do

Move At all. He has not moved since the Old Kingdom.
Rise The ritual forbids it until he has strength enough to break it.
Reach a Kindred mind he does not own He cannot dominate at range. He cannot touch the Prince of New York, or the coterie, or any Kindred who has not either put a hand on his sarcophagus or listened to the Chant. Mortals are another matter (above).
Search He is blind beyond the valley except through Ashcroft's eyes. He has no way to find anything, or anyone, without hands — and he has only one pair.
Act quickly Every effort beyond reflex is measured in years.

The design consequence. Everything the Architect accomplishes, he accomplishes through other people. Ashcroft is his hands. The Wakeners are his voice. The broadcast is his reach. He is running an operation out of a New York subway tunnel through a cult of thin-bloods because the alternative is lying in the dark, and he is not willing to do that for another four thousand years.

This is also the answer whenever a player asks the reasonable question — why doesn't he just take the Prince? He cannot. He can only take what is brought to him, or what listens.

Why Now, and Not 1905

He surfaced in 1905 and the Chant did not begin until the 1940s. Those forty years are not sloth. They are an ancient thing learning what century it is.

1905–1945: the watching. Through Ashcroft's eyes he received the modern world in sequence — motor cars, electric light, the Great War, radio, the second war. He had no frame for any of it. He had gone under in a world of river-valleys and mud-brick.

Two things came out of those forty years.

The hunger. He had the world once, for several centuries, and loved every night of it. He is not a creature weary of existence who has been disturbed. He is a creature who knows exactly what he is missing and has just been shown that it got better. The modern world is not alien to him. It is a banquet he has been made to watch.

The arithmetic. He also saw what four thousand years did to his King's blood: thinned across a hundred generations, scattered over continents, weak — and everywhere. There are more of Sekhemkhet's descendants in Manhattan than existed in the whole of the ancient world. Nothing in the modern night could stand against what he was. And every one of those descendants carries the only blood the rite will accept.

And the world handed him the tools. Broadcast radio. Later, cassette tape, so a thing that cannot move can still speak to people who were never in the room. A city of the discarded whom nobody counts or misses. He did not wait for the right moment. The right moment arrived, and he has been building ever since.

And then the curve. The Chant feeds on the wills it binds. For decades it took thin-bloods and Caitiff one at a time and gained almost nothing from them — but every bound will made the next binding easier, and by the 1990s it was compounding. In the spring of 1998 it crossed a threshold. The Dread arrived across the city in May, and the elders began to fall. (§1.4 — The Crescendo.)

For the first time in four thousand years, what he wants is close.

Voice

He is the most reasonable person in the chronicle. Play him that way.

He does not threaten. Threats are for people who might be refused and cannot bear it. He has been refused by the world for four thousand years and is still here.

He does not lie. He has no need to. The truth is already the strongest thing he has, and after four millennia of silence the opportunity to be understood by anyone at all is worth more to him than any advantage he might gain by deceit. If the coterie asks him what happens when he rises, he will tell them. If they ask what becomes of the town, he will tell them that too. If they ask who is coming, he will tell them.

He is courteous, and the courtesy is real. He was a high priest of a court that ran on ceremony. He will address the coterie with courtesy even knowing why they have come, because they are the first visitors he has had.

He is not sorry. Not about the thralls he drained, not about Ashcroft, not about the town, and not about what comes out of Saqqara. He can describe all of it accurately and feel nothing, because the capacity for that kind of feeling was the first thing the centuries took.

He will explain himself at length if permitted, and this is his one genuine weakness. He has not had a conversation since the Third Dynasty.

Register

"You are wondering why I did not simply take the man who rules your city. I would have. He never came. Nothing came. For four thousand years nothing came, and then a man with a lamp found his way through my house and put his hand on my door, and I have been building with that ever since."

"I am told there are machines now that carry a voice across an ocean. I have not seen one. I have seen photographs of one, through the eyes of a man standing in a room with it. Do you understand what I am describing to you? That is the whole of my century."

"My King is not a cruelty. He is a king. It did not occur to him to ask me, in the same way it does not occur to you to ask the stone in your ring whether it minds the setting. I have had a long time to think about this and I no longer believe he did anything wrong. I only believe I would like to be awake."

Through Ashcroft's mouth, in the Lodge:

"You will forgive the instrument. It has not been maintained. I have had no cause to speak aloud in some time and I find the apparatus has gone to rust."

"Sit down, if you like. There are chairs. This one bought them."

Things he never says

  • Anything boastful about his own power. He is describing a condition, not making a case.
  • Anything about Gehenna, prophecy, or destiny. He is not an apocalyptic. He wants to get up.
  • Anything that acknowledges Ashcroft as a person. He does not think of Ashcroft at all. This is the opening the coterie has, and he will not see it coming.

Speaking Through Ashcroft

He reaches one mind at a time, and only minds he owns — but Ashcroft has been his since 1905, and that is enough to give him a mouth.

When the coterie defeats Ashcroft in the Lodge, the Architect takes what is left of him and addresses the whole room. This is the only scene in the chronicle where every player gets to speak with him directly, and it should be used deliberately, because it is the group's one chance to meet the thing they have been chasing for a year.

How it plays

Ashcroft has fought them as a guard dog and lost. Then the ruin of him goes still, and the frantic thing that was in his face a moment ago is no longer there. When he speaks, the voice is his own throat and someone else's cadence: unhurried, formal, and ordered in a way nothing about Ashcroft has been ordered for a century.

He addresses the coterie with courtesy. He does not threaten them. He tells them, without irony, that it is already over, and that they may as well go home.

What belongs in this scene

The explanation. Why he is here, what was done to him, what he intends, and what it costs — including the Gathering, if they ask who is coming. All of it true. This is where the coterie argues with him — about the town, about the city, about what walks out of Saqqara — and where they find out he has already considered every objection and accepted it.

The advice. He means it. He sees no reason for them to die in his house for nothing, and he says so.

The blind spot, made physical. He will refer to Ashcroft as the instrument, or this one, or the man who dug — while wearing him, in front of people who have spent months reading his letters and standing in his rooms. He will do this without malice and without noticing. A coterie that has done its work in Broken Arrow has everything it needs to name the parallel, and this is the moment to try it.

What does not belong in this scene

The name. Kanefer is not spoken here. It belongs to the sarcophagus and to one person.

The offer. He does not bargain through Ashcroft. At this point he believes he has nothing to bargain for.

Any acknowledgement of the marked character. He knows what they are. He will not indicate that he knows. If the Storyteller wants one beat of ice in the scene, it is the moment his attention passes over that character and moves on.

Ashcroft's research. He does not mention it, because it has never occurred to him that it matters. (The Blind Spot, below.)

After

He withdraws when he is finished, or when the coterie proves tiresome, and he does not linger to see what they do next.

What is left standing is Lysander Ashcroft, for the last time. He spends the last of his will begging them to reseal the tomb and pointing them to his research. Then he is gone.

The Blind Spot

He did to Ashcroft exactly what Sekhemkhet did to him — took a mind, used it for a century, and never once considered that there was anyone inside it — and he has never noticed.

He is capable of describing his own four thousand years of suffering with perfect clarity and in the same breath referring to Ashcroft as the instrument. Not callously. He genuinely does not perceive the parallel. In his frame, a king may spend a servant; that is what servants are. He resents being spent. He has no concept whatsoever of being the one doing the spending.

And the harvest is his own. The King made only one childe. Every Kindred of the line the Chant is taking — every elder he intends to bleed at the entrance — descends from the childer the Architect made during the Roaming. He is spending his own children exactly the way his King spent him, and he cannot see that either.

This is the only place in him where an argument can get purchase. Not mercy, not fear, not the fate of the world — all three are things he has already priced in and accepted. But a coterie that puts Lysander Ashcroft in front of him as a man who was standing on that hill in 1758, who came back, who is owed something is doing something no one has ever done to him: describing him accurately.

And the place to attempt it is the Lodge, while he is wearing the man in question and referring to him as an instrument. The argument is available at the sarcophagus too, but it has a fraction of the force there. In Chapter Four the evidence is standing in the room with its mouth being used.

Whether it changes anything is for the table to discover. It should not be a guaranteed win. But it is the crack, and it should be the only one.

It is also why the research survives. He has never read Ashcroft's notes. It has not occurred to him that an instrument could find anything its owner did not. Everything the coterie needs to close the tomb has been lying on public desks in Broken Arrow for ninety years, written by a man his master never once thought of as a mind.

At the Sarcophagus

Everything above is preparation for a single scene, and the scene is not a fight.

The coterie comes down to reseal the tomb. The rite needs the blood of the King's line and a hand on the sarcophagus — the marked character's. The moment that hand touches it, he speaks into that mind alone. No one else hears anything.

It costs him enormously. The marked character's will is not his. He cannot command it; he can only persuade it, and holding an unbound mind open long enough to talk is the greatest effort he has made since 1905. He gets one conversation.

He did not send for this person and has no need of them. What he needs is that they do nothing — that the rite falters, that the blood is slow, that a line of Ashcroft's notes is misread — until it is too late: until the stragglers already in the valley come down and drag the coterie away from the sarcophagus, or the main body of the Gathering arrives and bleeds at the entrance.

He offers what he honestly believes he can deliver:

  • Exemption. When the others are spent at the basins, they will not be.
  • Their own will. It stays theirs. The sickness they have carried since Chapter One ends.
  • Standing. A favoured place in the new dynasty, at his side and his King's, above all the thinned blood of the modern night.
  • Everything else. Any pleasure the night can offer a Kindred, for as long as the night lasts — and, if they want it, a continent to rule.

He believes every word. He never lies. But the Spine already says what he is wrong about, and a sharp player may see it: nothing he is offering survives what walks out of Saqqara.

He gives them his name, which he has given to no one since the Third Dynasty, and that is the part that is actually designed to land.

Refusal does not anger him.

"Then finish your rite, if you can finish it before they arrive. I have waited four thousand years. I can wait the length of a stair."

Which is worse than a threat, and true.

Statistics

See Appendix C. As a general principle: the Architect cannot be destroyed, only sealed away, and he should never be resolved in combat. A coterie foolish enough to attack the sarcophagus of a fourth-generation Kindred learns this in thirty seconds, because he spends part of his strength to wake for exactly that long, and they do not survive it. 1.2 — SEKHEMKHET

The King. The Sovereign. The Sleeping King. Our Lord's Lord. Third Dynasty Pharaoh. Antediluvian. Third generation.

What He Is Not

Before anything else, a set of negatives — because every instinct at the table will pull the wrong way, and the chronicle collapses if the Storyteller plays him as a villain.

He is not the antagonist. He has no plan, no agents, no design on the modern world. He is not reaching out. He is not whispering. Nothing that happens in this chronicle is being orchestrated by him.

He is not awake. He has been in true, dreamless torpor beneath the Buried Pyramid at Saqqara — in the real tomb, hewn from the bedrock far below anything archaeologists have found — since the Architect completed the rites four thousand years ago. It worked. It is still working, though less well each night.

He does not know any of this is happening. Not the Chant, not the cult, not Ashcroft, not the coterie. He is asleep, and asleep for an Antediluvian is not a light condition.

He is not cruel. The centuries of rage the Architect endured during the roaming were not a punishment devised by a scheming elder. They were the raw output of a mind lying awake in a half-finished working, and they stopped the moment the seal closed. A king does not consider whether his high priest wishes to be spent, in the same way a jeweller does not consult the stone. The Architect himself has arrived at this conclusion and no longer believes his sire did anything wrong.

And he never appears. Not in a vision, not in a voice, not at the end. He is the thing the door is holding shut, and the chronicle ends before the door opens — or it ends because the door opened, in which case the campaign is over and something else begins.

What He Is

The consequence.

He is the reason the Architect cannot simply get up and walk out of that hill. He is what comes with him if he does. Every constraint in this chronicle traces back to a king who went down into the stone expecting his childe to follow him, and has been face-down in the dark ever since.

For the coterie, he should exist the way a mass on a scan exists: something inferred from its shadow, named only late, and worse each time they learn more about it.

The Stirring

This is the ticking clock, and it runs underneath the entire chronicle.

1905 — the crack. Ashcroft's hand on the sarcophagus disturbed the seal at the near end. The Architect surfaced into the half-state. At the far end, half a world away, something enormous shifted a fraction of an inch and did not wake.

The seal is shared. Whatever loosens one end loosens the other, and the Chant loosens it further with every voice added. The Architect is trying to work himself free. He is also, necessarily and with full knowledge, working his King free.

The Gathering would finish it. Ashcroft set the seal ajar; enough of the line's blood, spilled at once at the tomb's entrance, shatters it — and the far end goes with it.

The progression should be visible to the table only through the marked character, and only in the last third:

Stage What the marked character experiences
Chapters 1–2 Dreams of a chamber. Black stone, cedar, dry air. Always the same room. It is the Architect's tomb, and they will recognise it when they walk into it.
Chapter 3 The dreams acquire a second location, badly. Somewhere hotter. Somewhere with more room. They cannot hold it in memory on waking.
Chapter 4 Two rooms now, and the second is much larger, and there is something in it that is oriented toward them.
Chapters 5–6 The second room is the only room.

Nothing is ever explained to them. If a player works out that they have been dreaming two different tombs, they have worked out the stakes on their own, and that is worth more than any exposition available in the text.

Clan

Sekhemkhet is a genuine Antediluvian of one of the thirteen clans. Which one is a decision the Storyteller makes after characters are generated, and the decision should be made deliberately.

The rule: he must share a clan with the marked player character, because the marked character is marked precisely by carrying his blood.

The consequence, which is a feature: the Binding does not spread at random. Every Kindred it takes is of the same clan. But under the Dread, which hits every clan alike, nobody has seen the pattern (§1.4 — The Camouflage). The single-clan fact is the coterie's to discover.

Design note. The infection is a harvest, not a weapon. It spreads because the Architect needs the blood of the line, and the Chant is the only way he has to gather it. The political pressure comes the moment the coterie works out that one clan is being taken — and has to decide whom to tell, in a Court that will decide whatever it decides about that clan, which the marked character belongs to.

It costs nothing to run, because the players supply the clan themselves.

On choosing: any of the thirteen works mechanically. Clans with strong internal hierarchy or a reputation the Court already distrusts will generate more friction and are worth favouring. Avoid choosing a clan with only one player in it unless that player is the marked character — isolation at the table is not the same as isolation in the fiction. Whatever clan the table produces, the chronicle will be made to fit it; where official canon about that clan's founder gets in the way, this chronicle takes precedence.

If He Wakes

Not a scene to be run. A note on what the ending means.

He wakes hungry, four and a half thousand years out of date, in a world he has no framework for, with no plan and no allies and an Antediluvian's capabilities. He will not be a schemer or a dark lord. He will be a catastrophe with no more intent behind it than weather.

The Architect has thought about this and accepted it. He believes he will be at his King's side. He is probably right, and it will probably not be good for him, and he does not care.

For the chronicle: waking Sekhemkhet is a losing ending, but it is not a failure ending. It should be available, reachable, and survivable for the characters — for a while. What follows is Gehenna as this table would play it, and that is a different chronicle, if you want one.

At the Table

Never voice him. No lines, no dialogue, no whispers into anyone's dreams. The moment he speaks he becomes a character, and characters can be negotiated with.

Name him late. The King for most of the chronicle. The name Sekhemkhet should surface from Ashcroft's research in Chapter Four at the earliest — a real name, on a real cartouche, in real history, that a player can look up. Let them look it up. It is one of the few places in this chronicle where the actual historical record makes things worse rather than better: a pharaoh whose pyramid was never finished, sealed, and left.

The empty sarcophagus. Players who dig will learn that in 1954 an Egyptologist opened a sealed alabaster sarcophagus beneath the pyramid, in front of officials and the press, and found it empty. That chamber was a false one, cut to fool grave-robbers. It is not part of the real tomb at all, which lies in the bedrock far beneath it, and opening it disturbed nothing. Let the players find the empty box and draw the wrong conclusion first.

Keep him at one remove, always. The Architect talks about him. Ashcroft's notes circle him. The marked character dreams a room they cannot describe. That is the whole of his presence, and it is enough.

1.3 — LYSANDER ASHCROFT

The Ragged Prophet. The Tattered Prince. The instrument. Ventrue. Born c. 1733. Embraced 14 October 1758. Taken 1905.

The Only One Who Was Ever a Person

Everything else in this chronicle is ancient, or asleep, or was never given a choice. Ashcroft had choices, made them badly, and is the only antagonist the coterie can pity without condescension.

He is also the chronicle's most important source of information, its most dangerous encounter before the tomb, and the only argument that has any purchase on the Architect.

1733–1758 — The Ensign

Third son of a family with more name than money. A commission bought rather than earned, in the 42nd Highland Regiment, and a young man who intended to come back from the colonies with a reputation.

On the night of 14 October 1758 he was on Broken Arrow Ridge with Major Ferguson's company, eighty men raising a stockade over a flat clearing of black stone. He was present when the pickaxes struck the basalt. He was present for the fog, and for the twenty silent minutes.

He died there. Or near enough. Something found him bleeding on the stone in the dark and Embraced him for reasons that had nothing to do with what lay beneath — a passing Ventrue, a dying officer of good family, an opportunity taken. Coincidence, and nothing more. The Storyteller should resist every temptation to make his sire significant.

When relief scouts arrived three days later they counted seventy-nine bodies laid in a ring around the exposed stone — from the treeline, with the warriors still in the woods — and assumed the eightieth had been carried off. Nobody checked whether he had got up. When the Crown came back in strength, the bodies were gone, and they have never been found.

1758–1893 — The Ancilla

A hundred and thirty-five years in New York, and by the end of them a genuinely accomplished Kindred: well-connected, impeccably dressed, respected, with the Prince's ear and a reputation for rigour.

And underneath it, one question he never stopped working on. Why did the warriors come down that hill in silence? Why did the powder not take? What was under him while he bled? And where did they take the others?

He researched it the way a serious man researches anything — indigenous oral history, colonial land deeds, regimental records, the surveyor's charts, the trading post's original building plans. It took him the better part of a century to assemble a picture, and the picture said there was something under the ridge.

In 1891 he petitioned for domain. Six months of political warfare against a Court that could not comprehend why an ancilla of standing would trade Manhattan for three hundred mortals in a valley nobody had heard of.

He won. The Court concluded the clan curse had taken him.

He was going home.

1893 — The Cellar

He bought Greywick Lodge, went down into the root cellar, and cleared a century of dirt off a floor the settlers had never questioned. Hand-chiselled basalt. A faded royal seal.

He broke through, and found that the stone he had bled onto as a mortal a hundred and thirty-five years before was a roof.

1893–1905 — The Maze

Twelve years underground, and the finest scholarship of his existence.

The tomb is enormous: passages that double back, false chambers, sealed doors cut with hieroglyphs no one in the hemisphere could read. He mapped it. He translated it. He worked out, door by door, the rites that had closed it, and opened them one at a time. He measured every room against Saqqara. He was lucid for all of it, and he wrote everything down.

In 1905 he reached the burial chamber and put his hand on the sarcophagus.

He was overwritten on contact. It took no time at all.

1905–Present — The Instrument

Ninety-three years as someone else's hands.

He built all of it. The pacifying of the town was the Architect's blood magic, but everything requiring fingers was Ashcroft: the Medjay, the recordings, the tapes and the radio broadcasts, the copper rods driven into the bedrock, the abduction of drifters off the interstate, the recruitment of the Wakeners — and now, the preparations for the Gathering.

And he documented all of it, because he was a scholar before he was anything else and the habit survived the man. Broken Arrow is covered in his notes. He leaves them on public desks because the townspeople are furniture to him now, and because some part of him is still writing for a reader.

The decay is legible in three sources, and the Storyteller should distribute them across Chapters Three and Four:

The unsent letters (Post Office) 1910: formal, arrogant, mocking the Prince for doubting Broken Arrow's value — written five years after the taking, and he still sounds exactly like himself, which is the worst of it. 1938: frantic — the stone is singing, I cannot leave the cellar, send the Sheriff, I beg you — the last letter before the Chant work took him to New York. 1980s: no longer in English.
The recordings (Scriptorium) 1898, on wax cylinder: a refined scholar dictating his survey of the maze. 1940, on wire: a paranoid recluse. 1995, on cassette: a man weeping while a different cadence uses his mouth. Three generations of machinery, one voice coming apart.
The clinic chalkboard Years of failed work on why the Mohican bloodline would not take the pacification — the record of a man repeatedly failing to obtain permission to kill an old farmer.

TO BE WRITTEN — the title handout. Somewhere in the Lodge, late in Chapter Four or at the descent, the coterie finds the last coherent thing Lysander Ashcroft ever wrote.

When: late in the twelve years between the breakthrough in 1893 and the taking in 1905. He has mapped most of the maze, he is closing on the burial chamber, and he is still lucid enough to write. He has been checking the chambers against his own research on Saqqara and the measurements keep matching. Not approximating. Matching.

What he works out: this is not a tomb inspired by the Egyptian one. It is the same building. Someone carried the plans across the world in his head and raised it again under a hill in Greene County.

Form: three paragraphs of measurements and comparisons in a steady, educated hand — and then the last line in different pressure. Bigger. Faster. Pen dug into the paper.

He built the same room twice.

Draft the full handout when Part Five is written. Test the final line with and without the exclamation point; the flat full stop is colder, and reads as a man who has stopped being excited and started being afraid.

On the letters. They were never posted because he was never able to post them. He can describe the town, the excavation, his own deterioration, the weather. The sentence that would actually bring help is the one that will not come out. A hundred years of correspondence, and not one line of it is the line that matters.

What Is Left of Him

This is the part to get right, because the obvious reading is wrong.

He does not want anything. He is not protecting the coterie, and he is not defending a position. There is no position. He is a broken shell, overwritten in 1905 and fully dominated since, and what moves through the Lodge at night is the Architect's will wearing a Ventrue's coat.

There is no Court, no clan, no sire, no childe, no equal, no conversation. Three hundred mortals smile at him and mean nothing by it. Every night for ninety-three years the only other mind in his world has been the one wearing him.

And yet something of him remains — not enough to act, not enough to refuse, but enough to keep writing, and enough to spend exactly once. The whole of Ashcroft's arc in this chronicle is the coterie discovering that there is still a man in there, one moment before there isn't.

The Mute Place

When he talks at all, he talks in fragments of the scholar he was: 1758, the excavation, the maze, measurements, translations. Some of it is eloquent.

What he cannot get out is any accurate description of what it is to be held.

Every approach to it fails. The sentence starts and goes elsewhere — into measurements, into the maze, into the precise dimensions of a room he mapped in 1899. He does not appear to notice the swerve. He may repeat the attempt three times in a single speech and land somewhere else each time.

So the coterie is watching a man who has been consumed, and who is physically unable to describe the consumption. That is the tragedy, and it should be visible on stage rather than implied.

In New York — The Ragged Prophet

Chapters One and Two. Filthy, wild-eyed, unrecognisable as the ancilla in the Court's files, moving through dead stations and subway tunnels recruiting the discarded.

He is not seen clearly. Glimpsed at the edge of a platform, named by cultists, present in handwriting. The coterie should reach New York's end knowing his name, his face from an 1890s portrait, and nothing about what he has become.

He is a window. Whatever the Prophet looks at, the Architect sees. A coterie that stands in front of him at City Hall station has been seen by the thing in the tomb — the only way, short of the valley itself, that it can see anything at all.

He leaves the city when the Chant reaches its crescendo. Not a retreat — his work there is done. The elders are nearly ripe, and he goes home to prepare the tomb for guests. That the coterie follows him is precisely what his master does not want.

Seed for Chapter Two. The highway ambush is a genuine attempt to stop the coterie reaching the valley, run by the Wakeners' ex-military ghouls. It is under-resourced, and a coterie paying attention should notice: every van, driver, and able body the cult has is committed to the Gathering. They are being stopped with whatever could be spared.

In Broken Arrow — The Watcher

Chapter Three. He shadows the coterie from the moment they cross the town line and he is not catchable. He knows the valley, he has a century of preparation in it, and he has the Architect's reflexive ward working in his favour.

He should be felt constantly and encountered never: a figure at the treeline that is gone when anyone looks properly, a door closing two streets over, fresh notes in a room the coterie searched yesterday.

Something is watching through him, measuring whether they can be turned back. And somewhere underneath, what is left of Ashcroft is watching too — whether they kill, whether they feed, whether they hold anything back. It costs nothing to run, and it matters in Chapter Four.

And the Lodge is being readied. Rooms aired. Cots laid out in the cellar for guests who sleep by day. The basins at the tomb's entrance scrubbed clean for the first time in four thousand years. The coterie can find the preparations long before they understand what they are for.

The Confrontation — The Cellar Threshold

It happens in the Lodge, at the top of the cellar stair, and not in the tomb. He surfaces at the moment the coterie moves to descend, standing between them and the hole in the floor — guarding the exact spot where he destroyed himself.

The Fight

He fights as a guard dog, because that is what he is. No speech, no bargaining. Whatever words come out of him are fragments — a measurement, a date, half an order in a language nobody at the table speaks. The Architect wants the coterie gone, and Ashcroft is the only body he has in the room.

He fights to repel, not to kill. His master sees no profit in bodies in his house. A coterie paying attention should notice they are being driven off rather than destroyed. They win anyway.

The Borrowed Mouth

When Ashcroft is beaten, he goes still. The posture changes. Something else is using what is left of him, and it addresses the coterie — courteously, honestly — to tell them it is already over, and that they should go home.

This is the §1.1 scene — the group's one direct encounter with the Architect. Full structure in §2.17.

The Lucid Moment

The Architect withdraws when he is finished and does not linger.

What is left standing is Lysander Ashcroft, for the last time: confused, exhausted, ninety-three years behind, and aware of considerably more than anyone intended.

He spends the last of his willpower on a single thing. He begs them to reseal the tomb. He pleads. He grips a sleeve. He points them to his research — the study, the Scriptorium, the maze maps, a century of notes — and swears the way to close it is in there.

"The ritual to reseal it will be there. I'm sure of it. I'm sure of it."

He is nearly sure. It is not a secret he kept; it is a footnote. Somewhere around 1901, working out how the doors had been closed, he copied down the rite for closing the last one and never thought about it again — in 1901 he had no reason to want anything shut. It is at the bottom of a pile, part of the larger picture he was trying to unfurl. He remembers it's there. That is all he has left to give.

Then he is gone. Whether the body stays standing, burns, or crumbles is for the Storyteller; the man does not survive the moment.

What the coterie has done in the valley decides one thing: whether they believe him.

At the Table

He is the argument. A coterie that has read his letters, stood in his rooms, and watched his master wear him has everything it needs to describe the Architect accurately to his face. Ashcroft is the evidence, and in Chapter Four the evidence is standing in the room with its mouth being used.

He is the key. Everything the coterie needs to close the tomb is in his notes — the maze, the rites that opened it, and at the bottom of the pile, as a footnote he never thought much about, the rite to seal it again. It has been lying on public desks for ninety years because his master never once thought to look.

Never let him become sympathetic by being right. He opened the door. The plea at the end does not redeem the century before it. It is simply the last thing a man does.

Hold the 1758 reveal until Chapter Four. The players should have the battle, the man, and the Lodge as three separate facts for as long as possible. The moment they connect them, seventy-nine missing bodies stop being a historical curiosity and become the men he served with — still unaccounted for, on the hill he came home to.

1.4 — THE CHANT OF THE BROKEN CROWN

The Rattles. The Sickness. The Noise.

What It Actually Is

A sequence of phonemes, designed by the Architect and taught to the Wakeners.

It does not speak to the mind. No Kindred who hears it understands a syllable, and no amount of intelligence, scholarship, or linguistic skill will change that. A character may transcribe it perfectly and learn nothing.

It speaks to the Beast, and the Beast understands it completely.

That is the whole of the mechanism, and it explains everything else. The waking mind of a Kindred hears noise and finds it unpleasant. The thing underneath the waking mind hears something it recognises, from further back than the clan, further back than the Embrace, and it does not like being addressed.

The Crescendo

The Chant feeds on the wills it binds. Every Kindred bound to the Architect strengthens the blood-working woven through the phonemes and makes the next binding faster. For decades it crept: a few thin-bloods at a time, gaining almost nothing. By the 1990s it was compounding. In May 1998 it crossed a threshold, and the Dread arrived across the city at once.

The chronicle begins at the crescendo. The curve is steep and getting steeper. Every Singer silenced and every bound Kindred destroyed genuinely flattens it — the one real consolation the coterie gets in New York, and they may never know it.

Two Effects

They are separate, they run on different rules, and conflating them is the single easiest way to break this chronicle.

The Dread The Binding
Who Every Kindred without exception Only Sekhemkhet's line
What Terror with no object Progressive submission of the will
Duration Hours to days, then gone Permanent, and it accumulates
Visible? Immediately and to everyone No. It hides underneath the Dread.

And the first is the reason nobody has noticed the second.

Effect One — The Dread

Universal. Every Kindred in earshot, every time, regardless of clan, lineage, or how many times they have heard it before.

What it does. The Beast wakes and finds nothing to fight. There is no threat in the room, no fire, no sun, no predator — and it rouses anyway, hard, in response to something the conscious mind cannot locate. The Kindred experiences this as sourceless terror: a conviction that something is badly wrong, arriving with no object attached to it.

Frenzy checks with no trigger. This is the mechanical signature and it should be used generously. A character exposed to the Chant rolls against frenzy for nothing — no insult, no blood, no flame. Players will look for the cause. There isn't one. That is the horror, and it is happening to several hundred Kindred across the five boroughs on any given night this summer.

It lingers. Hours for the young. Days for the old. During that time: sleep is poor, tempers are short, frenzy comes easier and for smaller reasons, and a great many Kindred are making worse decisions than they would otherwise make.

Generation governs severity, and it runs the opposite way to everything else.

Elders and Primogen Worst affected by a considerable margin. More blood, older blood, a Beast with centuries of accumulated weight — and all of it screaming at nothing for days at a time. A Primogen who has heard the Chant once is not right for a week.
Ancillae Badly shaken. A night or two of it.
Neonates Unpleasant, disorienting, over by the following evening.
Thin-bloods Almost nothing. Their Beast is too faint to be frightened.
Ghouls A faint echo of it, through borrowed vitae: a bad few minutes, a short temper, nothing more. Enough to make the retinues on the mezzanine flinch; too little to stop the Wakeners' ghouls driving, engineering and dubbing tapes beside the Chant every night.

This is why the elders are coming apart and the gutter is not, and it is precisely the opposite of what the Camarilla's instincts predict. Power, in this one respect, is a liability, and nobody in the Court is capable of considering that.

For thin-bloods, it is a banner. Whatever unease they felt the first time wore off months ago. Now the Chant is simply the sound of where to go — audible, unmistakable, and safe to stand next to when nobody else in the city can bear to be within a block of it.

Effect Two — The Binding

Sekhemkhet's line only. No exceptions, no partial cases, no other clan affected at any exposure.

And it is not experienced as an affliction. Underneath the Dread — which they are feeling as well, along with everyone else — something else is happening quietly, and the Kindred it is happening to generally cannot tell. There is no moment of onset. There is no sensation that distinguishes it from the general awfulness of this summer.

The four stages

The mechanism is fixed. How it presents is not.

Stage One — Resonance. Vitae tastes of copper and dust. Feeding satisfies less than it should. (Kindred of every clan complain about feeding this summer, but only the line tastes copper and dust. Every rumour that mentions it comes from one clan — a hidden clue for a coterie that asks who is saying it.)

Stage Two — Intrusion. Something other than the character begins to occupy the edges of their existence. Lost minutes. Things known without having been learned.

Stage Three — Preference. The will begins to belong to someone else. Not possession — preference. The subject finds they want to go north, or to stay out past dawn, or to be near the people who sing. They can still refuse. Refusing is exhausting. They do it less each night.

Stage Four — Bound. A Wakener (§1.5) — and, when the call comes, a pilgrim.

How it presents

Differently in every single case, and the Storyteller should vary it deliberately, because the variation is what keeps the pattern hidden.

The ravers Violent, public disintegration. Frenzy, incoherence, destroyed havens. These are the cases the Court hears about, and they look nothing like an epidemic — they look like individuals going mad.
The strange Behaviour that is simply off. A reversed decision, an appointment missed, a walk to Queens at four in the morning. Mistaken for eccentricity, for politics, for age.
The dreamers Visions of a chamber: black stone, dry air, cedar. Always the same room. Months before they have any reason to know what it is.
The prophets Visions of the end of everything. Fire, the dead rising, the old ones walking. Dismissed as Gehenna hysteria — of which there is always a supply — and therefore never investigated.
The quiet Nothing visible at all. They go to ground. They do not come back.

Nobody manifests all five, and no two cases match. Which is exactly why a city full of clever, paranoid, well-informed Kindred has been looking directly at a single-clan affliction for two months and has seen five unrelated problems.

Rate

Thin-bloods and Caitiff of the line Days to weeks. Nothing in them resists it — no Beast loud enough to object. They are bound before anyone notices they were ill.
Neonates Weeks to months.

Willpower delays and does not prevent. A character may hold a stage for a scene or a night. Nothing available to a player character stops the progression while exposure continues.

What the Bound Are For

Ammunition. The Binding is not a weapon and not an army. It is a harvest. Every bound Kindred of the line is vitae the rite will accept, and the Architect is waiting until he has enough of the strong kind — elders, Primogen, centuries of blood — to spill at once. (The Gathering — §V, §2.22.)

The thin-bloods and Caitiff bound in days are worth almost nothing in the basins. The elders taken slowly are the whole point. This is why the city's crisis is worst at the top: the harvest is being taken from the top.

How the Bound Behave

Not as puppets. The Architect has no attention to spare for a thousand hands. A bound Kindred carries a single standing preference — toward the tomb, toward the singers, north — and otherwise goes on as before: articulate, credible, and in post.

When the call comes, they go, like sleepwalkers with one destination. They ignore the coterie entirely unless someone stands between them and the tomb — and then they fight with total, silent commitment, and recognise no one. A Primogen the players know may walk past them on a silent street at midnight without a flicker.

The Camouflage

This is the most important paragraph in the section.

Every Kindred in New York is frightened, sleeping badly, and behaving worse than usual. That is the Dread, it is universal, and it is loud.

Underneath it, one clan is being quietly taken — and every case looks different from every other case, and every symptom has an obvious innocent explanation available, and the whole thing is happening inside a general atmosphere of dread that accounts for all of it.

The signal is real, consistent, and completely buried in the noise.

Nobody has pieced it together. Not the Primogen, not the Harpies, not the Sheriff. The people best placed to notice are the elders, and the elders are the ones the Dread is hitting hardest.

The Discovery

The coterie are the ones who work it out. This is a genuine chapter beat and the Storyteller should protect it — do not hand it over in a rumour, and do not let an NPC arrive at it first.

What makes it available to them and to nobody else: they are young enough that the Dread only costs them a night, they are outside the Court's politics, and they are doing the one thing no elder in New York is doing — looking at individual cases side by side without a theory already in place.

The route. Three or four afflicted Kindred, investigated properly, with nothing obvious in common: a raving neonate in Brooklyn, a Harpy behaving strangely, someone who simply stopped appearing. Different symptoms, different boroughs, different circumstances. And the same clan.

Confirmation comes from the negative case — two Kindred who share a haven and hunt together, where one is deteriorating and the other is entirely fine. It does not spread. It selects.

When it lands, the coterie holds a fact the entire Camarilla of New York does not have, and they will have to decide who to tell, and discover that being right is worth considerably less than they assumed.

How It Travels

The Architect cannot move and cannot reach a mind he does not own. Everything below is Ashcroft's engineering, built across fifty years.

Live voice. The strongest form by a wide margin. A Wakener singing in a tunnel does more in ten minutes than a week of broadcast.

Local radio. A handful of small New York stations, pirate and licensed. Weak, continuous, and it does not require anyone to be in the room. The Chant never reaches further on the airwaves than the city.

Cassette tape. The invention that made all of this possible. A thing that cannot move can now speak to people who were never there. Tapes circulate hand to hand through the Rack.

Subliminal broadcast. Woven low into late-night programming on two small stations. Marginal, deniable, and the reason the affliction has no geography the Court can map.

Street corners and platforms. Singers working the late trains and the station mouths, which is where most of the city's incidental exposure comes from — and why a great many Kindred have a bad week without ever knowing why.

What the Court Sees

A city of frightened Kindred, a handful of dramatic public breakdowns, several respected elders behaving unaccountably, and no pattern whatsoever.

The theories in Elysium — Sabbat, Tremere, an internal purge — are all attempts to explain a set of events that genuinely do not have a common shape when viewed from inside the Dread.

The Court knows something is wrong, and nothing more. Every elder in the city has had the bad nights, and every one of them knows the Court is coming apart. Nobody has connected the bad nights to the singing. Most of the Dread reaches the city through radios and tapes, with no singer anywhere in sight, so to the people suffering it the nights simply arrive, for no reason, out of nowhere. There are rumours of a cult in the tunnels, and the Court files them where it files everything that happens to anarchs, Caitiff and thin-bloods: under nobody's business.

Cold Storage is the first time anyone saw cause and effect in the same room, in the right order — and the only witnesses who kept their heads were neonates. The Sheriff, buried in elders falling apart, has no time for a nightclub incident involving the Rack's gutter trash, and is more than happy to deputise the coterie and be rid of it. What nobody knows — not the link, and certainly not the clan — is what the coterie will spend the chapter putting together.

Why the Court cannot act: the Kindred senior enough to act decisively are the ones the Dread is hitting hardest and the Binding is taking slowest and most invisibly. The Primogen are frightened, exhausted, making poor decisions, and several of them are further gone than that. The coterie is sent because they are young, clean, unimportant, and disposable.

The Marked Character

Same Binding, same position as every other Kindred of the line. They are simply early.

They are not chosen, prophesied, or special, and the Architect did not send for them. The Chant is taking them exactly as it takes everyone of the blood; the coterie simply reaches the tomb before it has finished. If the coterie is slow, the character arrives further gone. If they are very slow, the character arrives bound — and walks up the hill with the others.

Their progression accelerates with every mile north. Do not explain why.

As they slide toward Stage Four, they begin to see the Lodge. A roofline through the pines. A lit window where there are only trees. No one else sees it. The ward is beginning to recognise them as his. Deliver it privately, and only once they are close to the edge.

Their dreams are the chronicle's clock. (§1.2 — the four-stage progression from one room, to two rooms, to the second room only.)

At the sarcophagus, their will is still their own, and that is the only reason the Architect has to talk to them at all. He cannot command a mind he does not own. He can only persuade it — and the coterie needs that same mind, and that same blood, to close the tomb. (§1.6, §2.21.)

Running it. Deliver their symptoms privately: a note, a message between sessions, a quiet word. Never in front of the table. The rest of the coterie should learn what is happening to their friend the way people actually learn such things — by noticing, badly, late, and after several opportunities to have noticed sooner.

The Sound

Multi-tonal, as though several throats were producing several pitches at once, which is what it sounds like even when one person is singing it. The phonemes are wrong for a human mouth — too far back, too many stops, vowels arriving from the wrong direction.

It does not echo. In a tiled station it should ring for whole seconds. It stops dead.

And the Beast hears it before the ears do. Half a second of the vitae moving on its own, of something underneath turning over and bracing — and then the noise catches up.

1.5 — THE WAKENERS

"We are the Wakeners. You sleep in your silk. Our Lord is opening His eyes."

What They Are

A congregation of the Camarilla's discards: thin-bloods, Caitiff, abandoned childer, ghouls whose domitors stopped coming, and a few ancillae who fell far enough that nobody noticed them landing.

They have been told they are the vanguard of a new order and that they will stand first in it, above the elders who never learned their names.

They believe it completely, and they are going to die for it, and it is not true.

Why It Works

The pitch is not sophisticated. It does not have to be.

Every grievance the Ragged Prophet names is real. The Camarilla does treat thin-bloods as a contamination. Caitiff are the joke in every Elysium. The Rack's discards are fed on, used, and stepped over by Kindred who have held their positions for three hundred years and intend to hold them for three hundred more. Nobody at that Court has ever offered any of these people anything.

Ashcroft offers them primacy — and he is the first person in their unlives to offer them anything at all.

Play them as converts, not as victims. The horror is not that they were tricked. It is that the offer was the best one available, and that the Camarilla made it so.

Two Tiers, and the Ghouls

The Bound. Kindred of Sekhemkhet's line who have reached Stage Four. (§1.4) They are the core and the majority, and most of them are Caitiff who never knew whose blood they carried until it began to ring.

Note the order, because it matters: they were recruited first and bound afterward. They came because they believed. The Chant only made it permanent. Their faith was chosen, and that is far worse than compulsion.

The Believers. Not of the blood, and therefore never called — present entirely on the strength of the promise. They can leave at any time. They do not leave. They are indistinguishable from the Bound in every respect except that nothing whatever is holding them.

A coterie that works out the distinction has found the most disturbing fact in the New York chapters.

Note on the thin-bloods. Whether Bound or Believer, they are the only Kindred in the city who can comfortably stand next to the Chant while it is being sung. Their Beast is too faint for the Dread to reach. (§1.4) Every other vampire in New York finds the sound unbearable; the discarded find it welcoming. They have drawn the obvious conclusion about who it was meant for.

The Ghouls. Mortal, attached to Bound Kindred, and used for daylight work: transport, rented rooms, carried equipment, the interstate abductions — and, when the call comes, driving the bound north for the Gathering in rented vans through the daylight hours. Some are ex-military, and those are the ones running the highway ambush in Chapter Two.

Structure

There isn't much of one, and that is deliberate — Ashcroft built it so that losing a cell costs him nothing.

Cells of four to nine, meeting in dead stations, service tunnels, condemned buildings, and the back rooms of businesses that stopped being businesses years ago. Cells know their own members, one contact above, and nothing else.

Lieutenants. A dozen or so across the five boroughs, mostly Bound, mostly long-term. They hold the Chant, run recruitment, and are the highest rank any player character will meet in New York.

The Prophet. Glimpsed, named, and never available. (§1.3)

Our Lord. Discussed constantly. Not one of them has the faintest idea what He actually is, and they do not consider this a gap.

Their Vocabulary

Worth using consistently; it does a lot of characterisation for free.

The Sovereign The same, used specifically to insult the Prince of New York.
The Sleeping King The same again. They have conflated master and servant completely, and nobody has corrected them.

How They Fight

They do not flinch, and they do not disengage. Rank means nothing to them — a player character invoking the Traditions, the Prince, or the Camarilla itself will be laughed at. That is the point. Those things were never theirs.

They will trade their existence for a delay. Not a tactic; an expression of belief. They are going to be first in the new order and the new order is coming regardless, so three minutes bought at the cost of one body is an obviously good trade.

They cover extraction above all. Preserving a Singer or a lieutenant is worth any number of the rest, and a cell will visibly spend itself to get one person out of a room.

They are not good at this. No training, poor equipment, no Disciplines worth the name. In a straight fight a competent coterie beats them decisively, every time, and should — and the bodies pile up, and more arrive next week, and none of it slows anything down. The despair in Chapter One comes from winning repeatedly and gaining nothing.

Statistics, gear options and the rules every Wakener follows are in Appendix C.1.

What They Actually Are

They are not a priesthood, an army, or a vanguard.

They are a supply line.

The Architect needs the blood of the King's line — a great deal of it, old and strong — delivered to a hole in the ground two hundred miles from the city. He cannot move and has no hands. The Wakeners carry the Chant into rooms, keep it sounding until the elders are taken, and at the end they will drive the harvest north.

New York is being destroyed as a side effect of a man trying to get out of bed.

The Wakeners do not know this. They will never know it. They think they are building a kingdom, and they are a supply line that sings.

The Lie

They will not be first in any new order.

The Architect has no use for thinned blood and no concept of a hierarchy that includes it. When he rises, the Wakeners are what they have been since the day they were recruited: material. The Bound among them will bleed at the basins beside the elders they despise, and add almost nothing. The Believers will simply be left behind.

Ashcroft knows. He made the promise because he was told to, in language chosen to work on exactly these people, and he has spent fifty years watching them believe it.

Whether the coterie tells them is a real choice, and it should go badly. A Wakener told the truth by a well-fed Camarilla neonate in good shoes hears the Silk saying what the Silk has always said: you are nothing, and you will remain nothing. They have heard it before, from better-dressed people, and they did not believe it then either.

At the Table

Give them names and faces early. A recurring lieutenant the coterie fights three times and finally kills is worth more than forty anonymous bodies. Let the players learn one of them well enough to be uncomfortable.

Let them be right about the Camarilla. They should be allowed to make their case, and the case should be good. The chronicle is stronger if at least one player quietly agrees with them.

Do not make them pitiable. They are fanatics, they are dangerous, and they have done monstrous things — the interstate abductions are theirs. Pity is the wrong register. What they should generate is recognition.

And they are the Architect's only working limb. Every Wakener the coterie destroys is genuine progress. It is simply progress measured against something that has been waiting four thousand years and can afford to lose all of them.

1.6 — THE MARKED CHARACTER

PROVISIONAL. This section cannot be finished until characters exist. What follows is the mechanism and the intent; the specifics — clan, symptoms, thresholds, pacing — get written once there is a player in the chair. See §1.2 on choosing Sekhemkhet's clan, and §1.4 on how the Chant treats them differently.

The Volunteer

Offered at session zero, to anyone who wants it. If more than one volunteers, roll.

What they are told:

Your character will be fighting a second, largely invisible battle alongside the main story of the chronicle. You will have extra challenges. You may also be able to bring insight to the story that nobody else can. Your character can be lost permanently.

What they are not told: anything else.

The Table Knows

This is the structural choice that makes the rest work.

Everyone at the table knows there is a marked player. It is not a secret that someone is carrying something. What is secret is what it means, where it is going, and — critically — how much of what that character receives is being shared.

So the coterie spends a year in a position the Storyteller never has to engineer: they know their friend is getting information they are not getting, and they have no way to tell whether they are hearing all of it.

Two Channels

Open. Delivered in front of everyone. Visions that arrive mid-scene, physical symptoms, the moments the character loses. The group watches it happen and reacts in real time. This is most of it, especially early.

Closed. Notes passed across the table, messages between sessions, a quiet word before play. Dreams belong here. So does anything the character could plausibly conceal, and everything at the sarcophagus.

The principle: the open channel makes the group afraid for them. The closed channel makes the group uncertain about them. The chronicle needs both, and the ratio should shift from open toward closed as it proceeds.

What the Curse Does

Four rough movements, to be made precise later.

Early — noise. Vitae tastes wrong. Poor sleep. Irritability with no cause. Nothing a player would report to the group as significant, and nothing that looks like information.

Middle — the room. Dreams of the Architect's chamber: black stone, dry air, cedar. Always the same room, recurring for months, long before they have any reason to know what it is. They will recognise it when they walk into it. (Full four-stage dream progression in §1.2.)

Later — intrusion. Waking moments that are not theirs. Lost minutes. Fragments arriving in the middle of scenes: a phrase in a language they do not speak, a direction they suddenly know is correct, the certainty that something is about to happen a second before it does.

Late — orientation. Something at the other end of the connection begins to face them.

The Marked Character as a Source

Some clues arrive only through them. This is what makes the position worth volunteering for, and what makes it dangerous.

The resonance is a connection, and connections carry in both directions. Where the coterie is stuck, the marked character can occasionally supply something nobody else could have: a direction, a name, the layout of a room they have never entered, the knowledge that a door is not where it appears to be.

And it is never free, never on demand, and never entirely trustworthy. It arrives when the Architect is exerting himself, which means it arrives when he is closest and the character is most exposed.

The bind this creates. The coterie comes to rely on information that is only available because their friend is being eaten. Every time they use it, they are choosing to keep using them. That is the moral weight this chronicle has been missing, and it belongs to the whole table rather than to one player.

Early, Not Chosen

The character is not special. They are of the line, the Chant is binding them like everyone else, and they reach the tomb with their will intact only because the coterie got there first. Speed is the whole of their protection, and it is the coterie's to spend.

They Can See the Door

Late in the Binding, the ward starts treating them as the Architect's own, and they catch glimpses of the Lodge that no one else can. If Gideon is dead, this is how the coterie gets in: the marked character, with Gideon's map in hand, looks where the map says and names what they see. It works. It also tells everyone at the table how far gone their friend is. (§1.7 — The Ward.)

Both Sides Need Them

At the sarcophagus, the character becomes the centre of the endgame from two directions at once.

Their friends need them to bleed. The rite to reseal the tomb, pieced together from Ashcroft's notes, needs the blood of the King's line and a hand on the sarcophagus.

The Architect needs them to do nothing. He cannot command them. He can only offer — and what he offers is everything. (§1.1)

It is the choice Kanefer refused four thousand years ago: lie down so the King can sleep, or keep living.

What they can do is open and entirely theirs: bleed, and close it; falter, and let the stragglers reach them; misread a line of Ashcroft's notes; argue for waiting; offer someone else's blood; or take the offer and say nothing. The rest of the table cannot tell sabotage from a mistake, and the two channels keep it that way until the end.

To Resolve Once Characters Exist

  • Sekhemkhet's clan — must match the marked character (§1.2)
  • Whether other players' characters share that clan, and what that does to the Court's suspicion of them
  • The symptom schedule: what arrives in which chapter, and what it costs mechanically
  • Willpower, Humanity, and frenzy interactions
  • The price of the reseal: Final Death, long torpor, or a heavy but survivable cost (recommended: long torpor, echoing the original rite)
  • Whether the reseal requires the bleeder's consent
  • Whether another Kindred of the line can pay instead — a captured bound elder, or another player character of the clan (recommended: yes)
  • What happens if the marked character dies before Chapter Six
  • Whether a second character can be marked if the first is lost

1.7 — RUNNING THE CHRONICLE

The Doctrine

This chronicle has a very large body of hidden information and no scene in which it is delivered. That is the design, and it is the thing most likely to break under pressure at the table.

Nobody in this story explains it. Ashcroft cannot. The Wakeners do not know. Gideon knows a folk memory with the reasons worn off. The Architect will explain a great deal, accurately, in Chapter Four — and by then the coterie should already have three quarters of it, so that what he provides is confirmation and emotional weight rather than exposition.

Two rules.

Show, do not read. Four fifths of what the players learn should arrive as objects, rooms, recordings, behaviour, and things that are wrong in ways they have to name themselves. A mural that stops halfway. Cold coffee in a full mug. A mileage log reading 0.1 miles. Seventy-nine bodies laid out in a ring.

The three-sentence rule. Any document the players actually read is capped at three sentences. A breadcrumb, never the loaf. The exceptions are deliberate and few: the title handout (§1.3), and the Architect's own speech.

The Knowledge Ladder

What the coterie should be able to know by the end of each chapter, assuming they work for it. Nothing here is guaranteed; all of it is reachable.

They can know They cannot yet know
Ch. 1 — The Incubation That the Chant frightens every Kindred and takes only one clan — worked out by them, from cases, and held by nobody else in the city. A cult is spreading it. The name Lysander Ashcroft, and that he vanished in 1894 into a town called Broken Arrow. That anything is under the ridge. That Ashcroft is not in charge.
Ch. 2 — Highway of Shadows The cult is trying to stop them reaching Broken Arrow, and is short of the means to do it. There was a massacre at Broken Arrow Ridge in 1758. The ground there has a history of being defended. Why. By what.
Ch. 3 — The Town of Silent Smiles Something has held this valley for a century. Ashcroft did it, and something did it to Ashcroft. There is a structure under the hill. Egypt is involved. There is a lodge, and the whole town denies it. Something is being prepared for a great many guests. What is in it. That it is a copy. Who the guests are.
Ch. 4 — The House No One Can Find Everything except the offer: the two sleepers, the broken rite, the King, Ashcroft's 1758 death, the replica, the Gathering — and that Ashcroft's notes may hold the way to close the tomb. What the Architect wants from the marked character.
Ch. 5 — The Same Room Twice The tomb itself: the maze, the copy made real around them, and the reseal rite assembled from Ashcroft's notes. What the Architect wants from the marked character.
Ch. 6 — Blood at the Threshold — —

If they fall behind, do not correct it with exposition. Put another object in front of them. The valley is full of a century of Ashcroft's notes and there is always another one.

Pacing a Year

Thirty to forty sessions, weekly, with gaps. Some practical consequences.

The weight is uneven on purpose. New York is broad and slow — investigation, politics, a city coming apart. The road is short and violent. Broken Arrow is where the bulk of the chronicle lives and it can absorb as many sessions as the table wants to spend in it. The tomb is tight and fast. The sarcophagus is one scene.

Do not rush them toward the hill. Every week spent in the valley is a week of the marked character degrading, the Court fracturing by telephone, and Ashcroft watching. The clock is always running, so patience costs them something without the Storyteller having to impose anything.

The Gathering is the last clock. It is imminent, but not minutes away: moving a dozen ancient Kindred two hundred miles is a huge logistical effort. From the moment the coterie reaches Broken Arrow, the Lodge is being readied. Show it in stages: preparations during Chapter Three, then the first stragglers — a few bound Kindred arriving early and alone, dangerous enough to make the threat real — and the main body only at the climax.

Recap ruthlessly. Over a year, players will forget which of eleven documents said what. Open each session with two minutes of here is what you currently believe, and let them correct each other. Their wrong theories are more useful to you than anything you could tell them.

Keep a running clue ledger. Which objects have been found, by whom, and what was concluded. Over forty sessions this is the difference between a chronicle that pays off and one that quietly loses its own plot.

Personal Horror

The single most important note in this section.

The design risk of this chronicle is that it becomes an excellent detective story with no Vampire in it. The coterie investigates, deduces, and confronts — all of which is external. Revised is a game about what the Beast takes from you one decision at a time, and that has to be built in deliberately or it will not appear.

Where the cost lives:

Feeding on the road. Two hundred miles from any domain, no herd, no Rack, no permission. Chapter Two should make feeding a problem with a body attached to it.

The town. Three hundred people who cannot consent, cannot resist, cannot remember, and will not be believed. They are the only vessels in the valley. Every night the coterie spends in Broken Arrow, they feed on someone who is already a victim. Do not comment on it. Let the players notice.

The human wall. When the thralls interpose themselves, the coterie is presented with a choice between innocents and progress, and there is no clean answer. Run it more than once.

The marked character. (§1.6) Relying on information that exists only because a friend is being consumed.

Gideon. The only conscious mortal for forty miles, and the only one who can meaningfully refuse them.

Ashcroft's lucid moment. A man spending the last of himself to beg them. What they have done in the valley decides whether they believe him.

The reseal. It needs the blood of the King's line. The obvious source is their friend. The other source is walking up the hill — a bound elder, possibly someone they know, who could be caught and bled instead. Both are reasonable. Neither is clean.

Never make the moral cost a puzzle with a correct solution. Humanity in this chronicle should be lost by people doing reasonable things for good reasons under time pressure.

Tone

New York should be loud. Wet, crowded, overlit, politically vicious. Play it broad. It is there to be missed.

Broken Arrow should be quiet. The contrast is most of the effect — the first time a player steps out of a car onto a silent street with mown lawns and a smiling woman across the road, the tonal shift should land like a change in air pressure.

Use the uncanny sparingly. Two well-placed wrong details will frighten a table. Five in a row become texture and stop working. Pick the strongest per scene and let the rest be ordinary.

Under the hill, go cold and dry. Everything above ground is damp mountain rot. Everything below is bone-dry air, crushed cedar, and dust that has not moved in four thousand years.

Never

  • Never voice Sekhemkhet. (§1.2)
  • Never let the Architect lie. His honesty is the frightening part.
  • Never make Ashcroft sympathetic by making him right. He is right. It does not redeem him.
  • Never explain the Wakeners' error to them on-screen unless the players choose to try, and let it fail.
  • Never let a single NPC be the only route to anything. Gideon in particular. (The Ward, below — if he dies, his map and the marked character get them in.)
  • Never hand over the single-clan discovery. It belongs to the players, it is earned by comparing cases, and no NPC in New York reaches it first. (§1.4)
  • Never resolve the sarcophagus in front of the table. (§2.21)
  • Never let the main body of the Gathering arrive early. Stragglers, yes; the convoy, only at the climax. (§2.22)
  • Never let the sarcophagus become a fight. The Architect cannot be destroyed, only sealed away. (§1.1)

The Ward

What it is. The Architect's reflex — a sleeper's flinch that hides the door to his tomb. It does not guard the hill; anyone can climb the ridge. It hides the house at the bottom of it. It exists only since 1905: before that he lay in true torpor with no reflexes at all, which is why the settlers built their trading post openly — decades after 1758, once the valley's people had been broken, on the site of the company's lost camp — and why Ashcroft bought it, by then a large lodge, openly in 1893. Old records show a building there; so might an old woman's childhood memory.

What the coterie experiences. The ridge is a ridge: thick pine climbing to a sharp crest. The Lodge stands at its foot, at the end of a path, and they cannot see either — only trees. If they climb, space curves quietly around the Lodge and they walk straight past it. Use one or two tells per visit, never all of them:

  • The walk takes twenty minutes when it should take five.
  • They pass the same split pine twice.
  • One watch is a few minutes behind the others.
  • The ground is level, but everyone's legs ache as if they have been climbing.
  • Someone finds their own footprints heading somewhere they never walked.

Something is off, and none of them can say what.

Who it does not stop. Minds the Architect owns. Ashcroft, the Wakeners, the ghouls, and the bound see the Lodge plainly. A coterie watching from the road may see a convoy van drive into solid forest and not come out.

Auspex. It cannot pierce fourth-generation blood magic. It sharpens the wrongness instead: a seam in the air, a pressure behind the eyes, a place where the trees do not quite join. Reward the attempt; do not break the ward.

The clues. Scatter them across the town:

  • Ashcroft's notes mention the Lodge constantly and never describe it — I must bring this back to the lodge, the lodge study, the lodge cellar — and never mention the ward.
  • The townsfolk have never heard of a lodge. All three hundred of them, pleasantly, and nearly in the same words. That uniformity is the Architect's will showing through.
  • The missing maps. Every map of the town has been torn off walls and cut out of brochures at the Architect's bidding. Pale rectangles on the diner wall. A tourist leaflet with a hole in it. Someone wanted the town unmappable.
  • Gideon's map. The only one left, in his house.

Breaking it. Gideon is immune. His tribe's distant ancestors swore a blood pact with Kanefer to defend the tomb until eternity, and what they swore to defend was his sleep. The Pact still protects their descendants from everything that shrouds the tomb — and now that Kanefer wants to wake, his oldest servants are guarding his rest against him. Gideon knows none of this except as folk memory with the reasons worn off.

He breaks the illusion by acknowledging it. He points and says, "The lodge? It's right there?" — and it is. It stays broken for everyone who was there. Once seen, it cannot be unseen. Anyone who was not present still sees trees.

If Gideon is dead, the ward stays up. The coterie needs two things: his map, to know where to look, and the marked character, far enough into the Binding to catch the glimpses. The marked character looks where the map says and names what they see, and the illusion breaks as it does for Gideon. It works, and everyone at the table learns what it means.

When They Go Sideways

They will. Forty sessions is a long time and this coterie has a car.

If they leave Broken Arrow, nothing stops them. The Chant continues, the Court deteriorates further, the marked character worsens, and the valley is exactly where they left it. Coming back later costs them, and the cost is legible.

If they kill Gideon, the ward does not fail. They lose their guide, not their way in: the only map of the town is in his house, and the marked character, far enough gone, can see what it points to. It is slower, it costs more, and they arrive knowing less. (The Ward, below.)

If they burn the Lodge, they have destroyed a building standing on eighty feet of solid rock. The tomb does not care — but they may have burned the only map of the way to close it.

If they go to the Camarilla for help, they find a Court too fractured to send anyone and too paranoid to believe them — and they have just told a room full of Stage Three elders exactly where the thing in the blood is coming from.

If they attack the sarcophagus, the Architect wakes for thirty seconds and destroys whoever did it. He cannot be destroyed, only sealed away. Warn them through the fiction first; if they do it anyway, it happened.

If they call New York, this is what their contacts can tell them. By the time the coterie is in Broken Arrow, the Camarilla of New York barely exists. The Prince is unseen, the Primogen are gone or going, and the offices are filled by people waiting to see who is left. The Court notices the exodus only as it happens — elders missing from their havens, ghouls with rented vans — and is too frightened and too divided to act on it. If anyone is sent north, it is the Sheriff, alone and late, arriving in Broken Arrow as an unreliable wildcard at the climax. Most of this stays off-screen: the coterie is two hundred miles away, and should feel it.

If the marked character dies early, the Gathering is unaffected — it never needed them. The coterie loses the only blood of the line in their party, and the reseal needs another source: a second player character of the clan, if there is one, or a bound elder taken from the convoy. The chronicle continues. The ending is worse and it is still an ending.

The one thing that cannot be improvised around: the coterie reaching the sarcophagus with no blood of the King's line available to them. If that becomes likely, the Storyteller has several chapters of warning — make sure the convoy offers a way, and re-read §2.21 early rather than late.

PART TWO — THE CHRONICLE

CHAPTER 1 — THE INCUBATION

New York City

2.1 — INTRODUCTION

Storyteller's note. This section is written to be worked from, not read aloud. It is the opening address of the chronicle: what the characters already know, the city they live in, and the specific wrongness of this particular summer. Take what is useful, cut the rest, and deliver it in your own voice across the first half-hour of play.

I. What You Already Know

A primer for players new to the setting, and a refresher for everyone else. None of this is news to the characters.

You are dead, and you are not. You were Embraced by a vampire who drained you and gave back a mouthful, and you have not aged a night since. You drink blood — human blood, specifically and exclusively — and without it you become something that is only hunger. Sunlight burns. Fire burns. Most other things do not.

There is something living inside you called the Beast, and it is not a metaphor. It wants to feed and it wants to survive and it has no interest whatsoever in the person you used to be. Every night you hold it down is a night you get to remain yourself. Some nights you lose, and the thing that happens then is called frenzy, and afterwards you own whatever it did.

The Masquerade is the first law and the last. Mortals must never learn that Kindred exist. Not because they would panic — because they would win. There are eight million of them in this city and six hundred of you, and everything the Camarilla has built for five hundred years rests on nobody looking directly at it. Breach the Masquerade and the penalty is final. This is not negotiable and it is not rare.

The Camarilla is the institution that enforces it. A sect, a government, and a protection racket, depending on who you ask. It recognises seven clans as its own, holds most of the cities of the western world, and is run according to a set of Traditions that everyone cites and nobody entirely obeys.

A city is a Domain, and a Domain has a Prince. The Prince grants the right to hunt, the right to Embrace, and the right to remain, and can revoke all three. Under the Prince sits the Primogen — the elders of each clan, in council, whose advice is technically advisory. The Sheriff enforces. The Keeper of Elysium runs the neutral ground where Kindred meet and where violence is forbidden. The Harpies decide who matters, which sounds trivial, and is not.

You are a neonate. Embraced within living memory, holding no territory worth the name, and dependent on the goodwill of people who have been dead for centuries and are not sentimental about it. In the eyes of the Court you are young, useful, and replaceable, and you will be reminded of all three.

And below you there is a floor. Thin-bloods, whose vitae is so far from the source that they can barely be called Kindred at all. Caitiff, who have no clan and no sire willing to name them. They hold no territory, have no voice, and are permitted to exist because removing them would take effort. Every Kindred in this city steps over them. You have stepped over them.

II. The City

It is July, and the heat has not broken in nineteen days.

New York in 1998 is the loudest place in the world and it does not sleep, which is convenient, because neither do you. Eight million mortals are crammed onto a piece of rock at the mouth of the Hudson, and several hundred thousand of them are awake at any hour of the night — drunk, dancing, working late, arguing in stairwells, or riding a train under the river with their eyes shut and their wallet in their front pocket.

The city is also rotten, and everyone who lives here has made an arrangement with that fact.

There is a cleanup on, officially. Times Square has been scrubbed. There are new banners on the lampposts and a police presence on the corners that photograph well. Below that thin bright surface the city is exactly what it has always been: a hundred and fifty thousand felonies a year, precincts that take forty minutes to answer a call from the wrong address, and entire blocks in the Bronx and East New York that are policed in the way that weather is policed — observed, recorded, and not intervened in.

Corruption is not a scandal here. It is infrastructure. Inspectors are paid. Evidence is lost. There are two precincts under federal investigation this summer and nobody expects either investigation to arrive anywhere. A detective who wants to close a case and a detective who wants to keep his pension are frequently not the same detective.

For a mortal, the subway after midnight is a calculation. Which car, which end of the platform, which station has a token booth with someone actually in it. People do it every night. They do it braced.

For the Kindred, all of this is excellent.

A city that expects to lose people does not investigate losing people. A body in the water, a disappearance from a club, a man who walked into a station at 2 a.m. and did not walk out — the paperwork for all of this already exists and it files itself. The Masquerade in New York is not held up by cleverness. It is held up by the fact that the city has a category for everything a vampire does, and the category is crime, and nobody is surprised by crime.

The Rack sits downtown and west: converted warehouses in the meatpacking district, industrial beats, velvet ropes, sub-basements with no fire exits and no cameras. The music is loud enough to hide anything. Everyone is on something. Nobody remembers anything, and if they did, nobody would ask them.

Mobile telephones exist and are the size of a shoe. Pagers are universal. The internet is a curiosity that some of the younger Kindred are beginning to find alarming, and every elder in the city has dismissed it.

III. This Summer

Something is wrong, and it has been wrong since about May, and nobody can say what it is.

It is not an event. Nothing has happened that anyone can point at. There has been no breach, no war, no death that mattered, no edict. The Traditions hold. The territories are where they were. On paper it has been an unremarkable season.

And every Kindred in this city has spent the last two months frightened, and none of them can say of what.

It comes and goes. A night arrives when the Beast will not settle — when it rouses hard, for nothing, in a room containing no threat at all, and keeps rousing, and will not be reasoned with. It passes. It passes faster for the young. Afterwards there is a day or two of poor sleep and a short temper and decisions that seem sound at the time.

Everyone has had one of those nights this summer. Nobody talks about it, because there is nothing to report: I was afraid, and there was no reason, and then I was not. It sounds like weakness, and in this city weakness is currency.

So the whole of Kindred society is quietly on edge, and it is making everything worse, and everyone has privately concluded that it is only happening to them.

The rest of the wrongness has three shapes, and nobody has connected them, because there is no obvious reason why they would be connected.

Something is in the blood. Kindred are complaining about feeding — not about scarcity, which would be ordinary, but about the blood itself. The complaints have been dismissed three times, as nerves, as a bad herd, and as territorial slander, and they have not stopped.

Something is wrong with the old ones. Nothing dramatic enough to name. A decision reversed. A meeting left early. An appointment missed. Individually, each of these is nothing at all. The trouble is that there are now a great many of them, and the people they are happening to are not the sort of people things happen to.

And the bottom of Kindred society has stopped behaving like the bottom. The thin-bloods, the Caitiff, the discarded. Something has changed in how they hold themselves, and nobody has managed to articulate it, and everybody has noticed.

None of this is knowledge. It is atmosphere. Anyone who wants more than atmosphere is going to have to go and ask.

IV. The Court

The Prince of New York has held the domain for a long time and is not a reformer. The city is run through exactly the machinery you would expect: territory, obligation, favour, and the knowledge that everyone owes somebody.

It has not been running well this summer.

The Primogen have met four times since May, which is twice the usual, and two of those meetings ended early. No account of why has been offered and none has been requested.

The Sheriff is working. Visibly, constantly, and with a distraction that does not suit him.

And the Prince has not held open court in three weeks. Business is being conducted through intermediaries. The official position is that the Prince is attending to a matter of some delicacy. The unofficial position varies by borough and none of the versions are good.

Run the Court as a body that has lost its nerve. Not collapsed — every office is filled, every formality observed, every ritual performed on schedule. It is simply that nothing gets decided, and everyone has noticed, and nobody will be the first to say so.

V. Tonight

It is a Friday in July, a little after midnight, and the coterie is in a converted warehouse on the far west side, where the music is loud enough to hide anything.

Proceed to §2.2 — Cold Storage.

Closing note for the Storyteller. The feeling to leave the table with at the end of this opening is not fear. It is the specific discomfort of a room full of very old, very powerful people all pretending nothing is happening, while the least important members of their society quietly stop being afraid.

Something is brewing. Nobody knows what it is. And the people who ought to be handling it are the people it is already inside.

2.2 — COLD STORAGE

Storyteller's note. Chapter One opens in a nightclub. The coterie is in the room when the Wakeners arrive, and they live through the Dread before anyone has told them it exists. Everything they do in New York afterwards is an attempt to understand what happened to them that night. This section covers the opening night; the open city that follows is §2.3–§2.7, and the rumour banks are §2.8.

I. Cold Storage

A converted meat locker on the far west side, in the middle of the Rack. The refrigeration was ripped out years ago. The name stayed because the owners thought it was funny.

Two floors.

The ground floor is for mortals. Unaffiliated, uninvited, five hundred of them on a Friday: a concrete dance floor under the old steel carcass hooks, industrial beats, strobes, heat you can lean on. Everyone is on something. The DJ booth hangs above the dance floor on chains and steel rigging, reached by a service ladder and a narrow catwalk — the centrepiece of the room.

The second floor is a mezzanine that runs around three sides of the room, overlooking the dance floor and level with the booth. It is closed to anyone who has not been invited: Kindred, and the mortals who belong to them — ghouls, hangers-on, posses, and retinues. Low tables, a private bar, a velvet rope at the top of the stairs, and a bouncer who knows exactly who he works for. The coterie has a table at the rail. From there they have the best view in the building.

A dozen Kindred on the mezzanine tonight. The club is the feeding ground of an ancilla of the King's clan, who holds it by the Prince's grant and lets clanmates hunt there. So most of the Kindred up there — the host, her guests, the regulars — are of Sekhemkhet's line. The coterie will not know that for months. The Wakeners know it now.

Why the coterie is here is best settled at character creation: part of how they know one another, a favour owed to the host, a message to deliver, or simply that Cold Storage is where neonates go on a Friday.

Running it. Let the first hour be ordinary. Introductions, feeding, a little Court gossip, a Harpy's hanger-on being unpleasant at the next table. The players should have time to settle into the room before the room is taken away from them.

The Clubgoers

Six regulars on the mezzanine the coterie can talk to before the raid and deal with after it. Each gives rumours in the first hour, comes out of the Dread changed, and opens a thread for the rest of the chapter.

Vivienne Sable — Toreador

The gossip. A fashion photographer who shoots the Rack's beautiful people and knows everyone's secrets. Catty, glamorous, always holding court at the best table.

  • Before: the easiest rumour source in the room, full of Court gossip about the Prince and the Primogen.
  • After: she spent the raid under a table, and is furious with herself for it.
  • Contributes: she is the coterie's guide to the witnesses. Ashamed of the table, and unable to bear being left out of the story, she attaches herself to the coterie the moment the Sheriff gives his order: "Rook? A dive on the Bowery, a block from CBGB — he's there most nights. Tuesday's on his bench in Tompkins Square at three every morning; he has been for years. Preston will be at 1 Wall Street pretending tonight never happened — I have his card. Nicky? Nobody finds Nicky. Nicky finds you." She had her camera tonight: her contact sheets show who was on the mezzanine — and, in the background of one frame, the shabby figures standing apart on the dance floor. She is also the protégée of the Harpy with the list, and the coterie's way in to see it.

Mister Tuesday — Malkavian

The colourful one. A thin, courteous man in a moth-eaten tuxedo and sunglasses. For weeks he has told anyone who will listen that the walls under Canal Street are humming. Nobody listens.

  • Before: he tells the coterie the humming is getting louder, and seems almost excited about it.
  • After: frozen like everyone else, he hums along with the Chant under his breath, perfectly in tune. Afterwards he is the first to point out that it did not echo, and he offers one unsettlingly accurate remark: "They weren't singing to us. They were singing to the blood."
  • Contributes: cryptic nudges whenever the coterie is stuck. Always right, and never in a way that is useful yet. He wanders out before anyone can stop him; Vivienne knows his bench.

Dominic "Rook" Alvarez — Brujah

The rabble-rouser. A bike messenger and Rack regular with a chip on his shoulder about the Prince. He picks a fight with the coterie within ten minutes of meeting them, then buys them a drink.

  • Before: anti-Court rants, and the Bank One rumours about the Prince.
  • After: he is older than he looks, and he frenzied. He comes back to himself on the dance floor with blood on his hands, consumed by shame and rage. The Singers are already dead — some of that blood is theirs — so he wants whoever sent them.
  • Contributes: muscle, and a sympathy for the discarded that gets him into the thin-blood squats and the den mother's soup kitchen. He is gone before the police arrive; Vivienne knows his dive.

Pretty Nicky — Nosferatu

The colourful one. A Nosferatu who loves club culture and goes out every Friday behind an Obfuscated face: a gorgeous club kid. Most of the mezzanine has no idea what he really is.

  • Before: the most charming person in the room, and a good dancer.
  • After: the Dread tears his mask away. For a few seconds, five hundred mortals below can see his true face at the rail — a Masquerade breach of his own, and he is devastated.
  • Contributes: his broodmate is the information broker who went silent in June (Bank Two, entry 9). Nicky is worried sick, and can show the coterie the way into the sewers. He vanishes before the police see his face; a few nights later, as Vivienne promised, he finds them.

Preston Whitlock — Ventrue

The cliché. A young stockbroker in a thousand-dollar suit, networking relentlessly, condescending to anyone without a title.

  • Before: pompous, name-dropping, trying to recruit the coterie into some favour-trading scheme.
  • After: he sat frozen and wept through the whole thing. He begs the coterie never to tell anyone.
  • Contributes: he bolts before the police arrive, desperate not to be seen; Vivienne has his card. At his office at 1 Wall Street, mortified, he wants the coterie's silence and will "talk properly" to get it. His mentor is the old Ventrue who chains himself in his basement during the Dread — the elder who knew Lysander Ashcroft at Court in the 1890s. Preston's embarrassment, applied at the office, is the leverage for an introduction.

Theo Castellano — [the King's clan]

The one they will like. Funny, warm, flirtatious, a Cold Storage regular and the host's favourite. He should be the NPC the players most enjoy talking to.

  • Before: he has been a little off lately — distracted, humming, tasting copper in his drinks. He mentions, laughing, that he keeps dreaming about the same room (Bank Three, entry 6).
  • After: he is calm. Far too calm. He stands, walks down the stairs and out into the street, and is never seen at Court again.
  • Contributes: a face for the Binding, and an emotional payoff. The coterie finds him again at the abandoned subway station at the end of the chapter, serene and singing with the choir. Once the clan is chosen, he is also part of the single-clan clue.

Also in the room: the host, the ancilla of the King's clan who holds Cold Storage, and her recently discarded ghoul manager, Eddie Quinn — the inside man who let the Singers rehearse, and who vanishes after the raid (found at the SRO flophouse).

II. The First Hour

This is the coterie's first chance to farm for rumours (Farming for Rumours, below), and to meet the clubgoers (above). Charisma + Empathy for the Kindred and their retinues on the mezzanine; Charisma + Streetwise for anyone who goes down among the mortal crowd. One roll per character.

Seed the room while they work. None of it should look important:

  • Four or five figures on the dance floor who are not dancing. Shabby, standing apart at even intervals around the room. Calm. Smiling slightly. From the rail they are easy to spot, and easy to dismiss. A player who asks is told they are gutter trash — thin-bloods passing as mortals, tolerated because they bring in the crowd.
  • The host. Gracious, tired, drinking more than she should. She had a bad night last week — everyone has — and she jokes about it too readily.
  • A cassette changing hands at the ground-floor bar, between a thin-blood and a mortal who does not know what he is holding.

III. The Booth

At two in the morning, the figures on the dance floor move at once.

They go up the service ladder and along the catwalk to the booth — fast, practised, clearly rehearsed. The mortal crowd barely notices. The coterie, at the rail, sees all of it.

The DJ turns around and one of them tears his throat out. Not neatly. Then they lift him over the edge of the booth and throw him down onto the dance floor, into the middle of five hundred people, still moving.

The music cuts. The strobes keep going. In the silence, a thin-blood with blood to the elbows leans into the microphone.

Make it visceral, and make it the coterie's view. The mortals below see a body fall and do not understand what they saw. The coterie, level with the booth, saw exactly what happened, and they are the only ones in the building who did.

IV. The Chant

They begin to sing.

Through the club's own speakers, the Chant fills both floors. Mortals hear an ugly drone, like a fault in the wiring, and start pushing for the exits around the body. The Kindred hear something else.

Every Kindred in the building notices, later, that it did not echo. In a concrete room built for echoes. (Bank Three, entry five, is now something they know first-hand.)

V. The Dread

The moment the Chant begins, every Kindred character in the building rolls Courage, difficulty 6. (§1.4 — Effect One: The Dread.) It is fear, not anger — but the Beast cannot run from a threat it cannot find. It can only lock up, or go for the one thing it can place: the source of the sound.

Success (one or more) Frozen. The Beast clamps down. Sourceless, crushing terror, the Beast awake and roaring at a room with no threat in it. The character cannot stand, cannot run, cannot intervene. They can only watch.
Failure Frenzy, aimed at the Singers. The mind cannot locate the threat, but the Beast knows exactly where the sound is coming from, and goes for it — over the rail, up the rigging, in a straight line.
Botch The same frenzy — but it does not end with the Chant (When It Ends, below).

With two to four dice of Courage, roughly three neonates in four will freeze. Expect one character in a typical coterie to go over the rail, which is the right proportion for the scene.

The agency valve. The frenzy rules already allow it: a player may spend a point of Willpower to control one action for one turn. It works in both states. A frozen character can buy a turn to grab a friend's arm, shout a warning, drag someone back from the rail — or go for a Singer deliberately, which is a choice they own. A frenzied character can buy a turn to rein in one act — not tearing through the ghoul who is in the way. Without it, the frenzy is ill-contained: it runs straight at the Singers, and anyone on that line is in the way. A neonate has enough Willpower to buy several turns. It keeps the players in the scene, and it tells the Storyteller a great deal about their characters in the first session.

Frenzied characters use the standard benefits: wound penalties are ignored, no Willpower roll is needed to accomplish a feat, and they are immune to Rötschreck.

The Singers are martyrs. Four or five of them, and they knew when they climbed the ladder that they would not climb down. They do not run, do not defend themselves, and do not stop. As each one dies, the Chant thins — audibly, one voice fewer — and the ones still standing keep singing over the body. They will trade their existence for a delay (§1.5), and tonight the delay is every second the sound lasts.

The NPCs do not roll.

  • The elders all fail, and get there first. Older, stronger, closer. The host goes first, then her guests — over the mezzanine rail and up the rigging, and they bring the booth down. Their straight line to the Singers runs through the crowd below and the tables beside the coterie's own; that is where the three or four mortal dead come from. The people above the coterie in every hierarchy they know lose themselves completely, a few feet away.
  • The ancillae split. Some freeze. Some frenzy.
  • Theo Castellano does not react at all. Calm, in the middle of all of it. That is a clue.

The killing is not done by the Wakeners. They killed one man to get a microphone. The Kindred they sang to do the rest — including killing the Singers, which is exactly what the Singers came for.

When it ends. The Chant stops when the last Singer dies — three to five turns, at the Storyteller's discretion. The frozen are released. An aimed frenzy ends with the sound, without a roll; the target is gone. A botched frenzy does not: it continues into the silence, on a mezzanine full of bleeding people, until the character succeeds at Self-Control as for any frenzy. That is the dangerous result, and it should feel like one.

Afterwards. The Dread lingers until the following evening for neonates: frenzy rolls at +1 difficulty, poor sleep, a short temper. Whatever a frenzied character did, they own; if they killed, the Storyteller may call for a degeneration check. A frenzied character who drank from a Singer has swallowed Kindred vitae — a seed to use later, or to leave alone. If they tore a Singer apart, there is a body: thin, shabby, smiling even now, with a cassette in its pocket.

The marked character, privately. Under the terror there is something else: the sound is familiar, as if it were calling them by a name they have forgotten. And the blood flooding the room below smells of copper and dust — to them, and to no one else. This is the first night of the Incubation. Deliver it on a note.

VI. After

Within ten minutes it is over. The Singers are dead, every one of them, in the wreck of the booth on the dance floor. The frenzied come back to themselves among what they have done. The host sits on the floor of her own mezzanine, unable to speak. Theo Castellano, calm and smiling, simply walks down the stairs, out into the street, and is never seen at Court again. Across the mezzanine: Vivienne crawling out from under her table, Rook on the dance floor with blood to the wrists, Pretty Nicky with his real face in his hands, Preston weeping, and Mister Tuesday still humming.

The witnesses scatter. Before the first patrol car arrives, they are gone: Rook out through the loading dock with blood on his hands, Pretty Nicky simply not there any more, Preston running for his car without a word, Mister Tuesday wandering out into the street, still humming. Only Vivienne stays — and the host, who cannot be moved. (The Sheriff, below, will want them all found.)

The Masquerade is cracked, not broken. Most of the crowd saw strobes, a fall from the booth, a fight, and a stampede for the doors — nothing they could describe, and nobody sober. Three or four mortals are dead in the crush and on the mezzanine, a handful more are hurt, and the city already has a category for all of it: a fight in the booth, bad drugs, a stampede. What is left is specific, and it can be fixed — the bodies with bite marks, the mortals at the mezzanine tables who saw too much, Pretty Nicky's face at the rail, and the 911 calls already going in. The coterie, freed at last, has minutes: to get the Kindred out, to deal with what the mortals saw, to decide what the police find.

The tape in the booth. The Singers' own cassette is still in the deck, in the wreck of the DJ booth. This copy is guaranteed; the Singers' bodies and the ground-floor bar give the coterie more.

  • Side A is the Chant, dubbed off the radio by one of the cells. Just before it starts there is the tail of a station ident — "…WKNA, thirteen-forty, Astoria's—" — and a loud man's voice, the shock jock, cut off mid-sentence. (The radio station.)
  • Side B is almost blank, except for a few seconds of an older recording bleeding through from whatever was taped over: a cultured, very old-sounding man's voice, reading measurements. Nobody will know what it is for a long time. (The elder's basement names the voice; the record shop explains how it got onto the tape — the cells reuse the Prophet's old cassettes as blanks.)

The staff. Questioned before the police arrive, a bouncer mentions that Eddie Quinn, the manager, was cut off by the host weeks ago and has been sleeping at the Hotel Marlowe, an SRO on West 47th Street, ever since. Nobody has seen him tonight. (The SRO flophouse.)

The bodies. As the coterie leaves, the coroner's van is backing up to the loading dock. The dead are going to the city morgue at Bellevue, and so is the story of how they died. The Sheriff will make that the coterie's problem. (Bellevue.)

And there is already a story that explains it. A mortal was killed in front of a room full of hungry elders, and they lost control. It is simple, it is plausible, and it is what the Court will choose to believe. Only the coterie knows the order: the singing first, the frenzy after, and a terror with nothing in the room to be afraid of.

VII. The Sheriff

He arrives before the first patrol car, with two ghouls in cheap suits who start talking to the mortal staff immediately.

He is frayed. He has not fed. His pager does not stop. He looks at the room for about thirty seconds and starts giving orders, and every one of them is about the breach: who was at the rail, which bodies have bites, which of the staff can be trusted to say stampede. The Masquerade is the only thing in the room he can see.

He listens to perhaps half of what the coterie tells him about the singing. He has already decided what happened: a kill in front of hungry elders, a blood frenzy, a mess. The singing does not interest him. Five hundred witnesses do.

The cover story holds. By morning it is a fight in the DJ booth and a stampede, with drugs involved. The Court's people in the precinct and the papers make it stick, and the city, which expects to lose people, accepts it. The loose ends — Dr. Kahn's autopsy notes, Detective Russo, a few mortals who saw the mezzanine — are what the coterie inherits.

He gives them the case.

"You were here. You saw it. Help my people clean this up — then it's yours. Find out who did it, and tell me when you have something worth my time. I have elders breaking habits they've kept for a century, a Court that can't agree on what night it is, and no time for gutter trash with a boombox. Elysium meets as usual at the Met. If you have anything, bring it there."

What he hands over:

  • The task. Clean up the breach, then find who did this.
  • The morgue. "The dead are going to Bellevue. The ME's backed up — they won't be cut open till Monday, three nights. When they are, the reports say stampede. Fix them or lose them before they're filed downtown. Not before Monday: you'll only make the doctor remember your faces." A Masquerade errand with a window: nights 3 to 5 (Bellevue).
  • Elysium. Meeting "as usual" at the Metropolitan Museum — the right place to report, and to be seen. When it is cancelled at stage two of the collapse (No Cavalry), the players will feel the floor move.
  • A token of authority. His name to drop, a pager number that sometimes gets a call back, and a dead drop — a loose brick behind a bench in Tompkins Square Park — for anything that will not fit on a pager. It opens doors in New York for now, and it will be worth less every week as the Court comes apart.
  • The witnesses. "Everyone who was on that mezzanine. Talk to them before they get their stories straight." Most of them are already gone. Vivienne knows where to find them.
  • The clear sense that nobody is checking their work.

He calls it unimportant. It is the most important thing happening in the city, and he has just given it to the least important Kindred in the room.

VIII. What the Night Establishes

The players leave Cold Storage with first-hand knowledge they cannot yet interpret:

  • The Dread, felt in their own bodies, with no cause they can name.
  • That it gets worse with age. They watched elders fall while they held on.
  • The Singers, rehearsed and serene, the DJ's death, and the sound that did not echo.
  • The order of events, which contradicts the story the Court will tell.
  • The cassette from the DJ booth — a station ident on one side, an old man's voice on the other — and perhaps more copies.
  • The coroner's van, taking the dead — and their bite marks — to the Bellevue morgue.
  • A missing manager, and the name of the SRO where he has been sleeping.
  • Nowhere to feed. The police close Cold Storage the next night. The coterie will need a new hunting ground.
  • A job, from a Sheriff who does not want to hear about it.

And one clue they will not recognise for months: of the Kindred in that room, the ones who later come apart for good are all of one clan. Cold Storage was not a random target. It was a clan haunt, and the Wakeners came to sing to the King's blood. (§1.4 — The Discovery.)

2.3 — THE OPEN CITY

From here, Chapter One is a city to explore rather than a sequence to follow. The coterie always has several places to go next, and they choose.

How the web works.

  • Leads are clues: something found at one location that points to another. Most locations offer one or two. A few are deliberate dead ends — atmosphere, politics, danger, and nothing to follow.
  • Doors are people. The clubgoers and the Sheriff can take the coterie somewhere at any time, without needing a clue. They are the coterie's safety net if the leads run dry.
  • Clues stay sparse. Roughly one location in three carries a real Wakener or Ashcroft clue. The rest are the city itself: Kindred politics, feeding, favours, and the Court coming apart.

The finale is gated, in the fiction. City Hall station needs two pieces of information — where (the sewers or North Brother Island) and when. "When" is a signal, not a date: the Prophet preaches on long nights, when the Sound on the radio runs on past three, and there has not been one since Cold Storage. There will be one more — his farewell sermon before he goes north — and the Storyteller places it at the Squeeze (No Cavalry, §2.7). Arnold Kess gets a day's warning by courier; Old Moses, Mama Celestine or any radio gives the signal on the night itself. A coterie that finds the station early finds it empty — candle wax, a broken-crown mark, nobody home — and staking it out is a perfectly good plan.

The identity is not gated. The elder in Brooklyn Heights can name the voice on the tape whenever they reach him, and shows them the young man's face in a photograph of the 1891 Court. The record shop links that voice to "the old man." The name is put to the ruined face only at the station.

2.5 — THE GRAVEDIGGERS

A small Sabbat pack, sent to probe a Camarilla city that suddenly looks weak. Their priest believes the Dread is exactly what it is: an Antediluvian calling its blood. The Sabbat exists to destroy the Antediluvians, so the pack is hunting the source too — the only Kindred in New York with the right theory, and the most dangerous people the coterie will meet.

  • Sister Benedetta — Lasombra, the priest. Calm, devout, terrifyingly lucid. She reads the Dread as scripture coming true. "Your blood is being called home. Ours is not. Think about why."
  • Hollis — Brujah antitribu, the ductus. Brutal and practical. He wants New York more than he wants Gehenna.
  • Mister Bone — Tzimisce. A fleshcrafter who interrogates captured Wakeners. You know where he has been by what he leaves behind.
  • Jinx — Pander. A clanless street kid, the pack's eyes, and the nearest thing it has to a human face. Possibly the one who could be turned or spared.

How they cross the coterie's path:

  1. Traces. Wakeners turn up dead and reshaped — under the bridge, at the flophouse, outside the soup kitchen. Someone else is hunting the cult.
  2. First contact. The pack has been watching the record shop for two nights; when Jinx sees the coterie go down the stair, Hollis decides the Camarilla is not having it. If the coterie never goes there, first contact happens at whichever lead the two groups reach together — and the pack, having watched them, is never there by chance.
  3. The truce. A matchbook at the Bowery dive — or from Jinx in person — and a parley at Coney Island. The same enemy, for one night.
  4. The station. The pack knows "where" from Tobias and listens for the same long night as the coterie, so they arrive at City Hall on the same night, and the finale becomes a three-way fight. In the chaos, the Prophet gets away.

2.7 — NO CAVALRY

The Court collapses in stages as the chapter goes on. Advance it roughly every two or three locations, or whenever the city needs to feel worse.

  2. Elysium cancelled. The Keeper of Elysium is missing.
  3. The Primogen go quiet. Meetings stop. The Harpy's salon empties of everyone except Isolde Varga herself, who is having the time of her life. The Prince is sealed in his haven.

The rule. Whenever the coterie asks for help, the answer is silence, an excuse, or someone too frightened to come. Never a flat no. Always something worse.

Rules for every Wakener.

  • Rank means nothing. The Traditions, the Prince's name, a title: worth nothing to them. Intimidation that rests on Kindred standing fails automatically. They never flee and never surrender.
  • The martyr's trade. Anyone in reach of a threatened Singer or lieutenant spends Willpower to ignore wound penalties for the turn (a core rule) and puts their body in the way. A cell will spend itself to get one person out of a room.
  • Hearing the Chant. Thin-bloods and ghouls never roll for the Dread. Caitiff and the Fallen roll Courage like any Kindred, but they have sat through it a hundred times: a success leaves them shaken (+1 difficulty to everything for the scene) rather than frozen, and a failure freezes them.

  • Poor, and it shows. Their gear is never better than the options below, and one good gun per cell is a lot. Only the ex-military ghouls have any training.
  • Winning gains nothing. A competent coterie beats them decisively, every time (§1.5). Threaten with numbers and with what they are willing to do, not with dice pools.

APPENDIX E — DESIGN LOG

How this chronicle was built, what was tried and abandoned, and why. Written for the Storyteller, and for anyone — human or otherwise — who picks this document up later and is tempted to "improve" something that was already argued out.

Rule for future collaborators: anything listed here under Rejected was considered seriously and discarded for a stated reason. Do not reintroduce it without first answering that reason.

E.1 — Origins

The chronicle began as a long brainstorm with another model under the title Symphony for the Red Dawn. That draft had strong bones — the clue-splitting on Ashcroft's name, the 1758 massacre, the coyote pelts paying off as the Medjay, the unsent letters — and a set of load-bearing contradictions nobody had challenged. The rewrite kept the bones and replaced the engine.

Rejected: the original title. It referenced a Gehenna-at-dawn frame that no longer exists in the story, and nothing in the chronicle is a symphony. Adopted: He Built the Same Room Twice. It is a sentence a character actually writes (§1.3), it conceals its meaning until the moment it lands, and it describes the Architect as he truly is — not a schemer, but a man who built the only thing he knew how to build.

E.2 — The Sacrifice Logic (four versions)

The hardest problem in the chronicle. It went through four full versions before it held.

Version 1 — the keystone seals, he wants to die. Rejected. If paying the sacrifice seals the tomb, it cannot also wake Sekhemkhet. And a Methuselah seeking oblivion can walk into the sun; nothing explained why he didn't.

Version 2 — he is deceived; he thinks he is sealing, but is opening. Rejected. Mechanically clever, impossible to communicate at a table, and it made the villain a dupe rather than an agent. Also required a "binding" whose purpose nobody could state.

Version 3 — the keystone is installed, not killed; he wants a substitute. Rejected. Closer, but still two interlocking mechanisms, and it made the marked character a chosen-one component — a tonal problem in a game about being small and damned.

Version 4 — the foundation. The King lay down to sleep. The rite required two sleepers, because a bond cannot be closed from one end. The childe didn't follow — not from fear, but from appetite; he did not want to stop living. Centuries of the King's wakeful rage drove him to build a replica and complete the rite from the far end. It worked. Both slept. Ashcroft cracked the near end. The childe now wants to rise, knowing his King rises with him, and has accepted that.

Why it holds: one rule — the seal takes two, and neither can wake alone — explains the torpor, the rage, the replica, the half-state, and why waking him wakes Sekhemkhet. The sin is vanity, which fits Vampire. And the motive is comprehensible: he would rather rule a diminished world beside a monster than sleep another four thousand years.

Version 5 — the Gathering (adopted). Version 4's rule stands, but it left the Chant's end goal vague: gather enough bound blood — how, and to what end? The answer: bound vitae of the King's line, spilled together at the tomb's entrance, shatters the seal Ashcroft set ajar. The Chant binds; the elders are the harvest; the Wakeners are the supply line; the Gathering is the ritual. He lay down in blood and will get up the same way. The marked character is not needed by the plan at all — they are an obstacle the Architect improvises around.

Rejected in the same revision: the Chant as a search for one descendant (it never explained why the fanatically willing Bound could not simply walk up to the stone); a strong-willed descendant sifted out by the Chant (edged back toward the chosen-one component rejected in Version 3); the Architect holding the marked character at Stage Three (no mechanism, and it made them special).

Rejected along the way: the suicide chamber. An early draft placed a chamber cut upward toward daylight as evidence of failed suicide. The Architect never sought death. He sought sleep, and later, life. Do not reintroduce it.

E.3 — The Architect

Rejected: the name Imhotep. Too famous; drags The Mummy (1999) into a 1998 chronicle. Adopted: Imhotep as a red herring. His name is on Sekhemkhet's enclosure wall, and players will find it. He was the mortal master of works history remembered; Kanefer is the servant it erased. Adopted: Kanefer, spoken exactly once, at the sarcophagus, as an intimacy rather than a reveal. A servant whose name has gone out of the world is more frightening than any syllables, and the contrast with a King whose name is cut into stone is the whole relationship.

Rejected: a deceived Architect. See E.2. Adopted: an honest one. He never lies. His honesty is the frightening part, and it is what makes the coterie's choices genuinely theirs.

His one vulnerability is the blind spot — he did to Ashcroft exactly what his King did to him and has never noticed. Mercy, fear, and the fate of the world are all already priced in. Only the parallel has purchase. It should be a crack, never a guaranteed win. It is also why Ashcroft's research survives: he never imagined an instrument could find anything.

E.4 — Sekhemkhet

Rejected: an active, scheming Antediluvian. An earlier conception had him whispering up the bond, orchestrating events. Adopted: asleep, unaware, uninvolved. There is exactly one antagonist with intent in this chronicle, and he is a servant trying to get out of bed. The King is consequence, not character. Never voice him. The moment he speaks, he becomes negotiable.

Rejected: mentioning Antediluvians or Gehenna early. The opening address (§2.1) originally included them in the primer. Cut: no Antediluvian is named or hinted at until the coterie learns who Kanefer's sire is.

E.5 — Lysander Ashcroft

Adopted late, and one of the best additions: he fought at Broken Arrow Ridge in 1758, died there, and was Embraced on the black stone by a passing Ventrue for unrelated reasons. His century of research is a man returning to the worst night of his mortal life. The Court thought he was mad to want a worthless hamlet. He was going home.

Rejected: a surviving soldier. It would break the massacre's "no survivors" detail. He died with the company; the scouts counted seventy-nine and assumed the eightieth was carried off. Rejected: a significant sire. Coincidence is deliberate. Adding a conspiracy there adds a fourth layer to a story that was hard-won in its simplicity.

Why he fights the coterie went through three versions:

  • Protecting them from the tomb — rejected; too soft, and it raised the question of what happens if they simply listen.
  • Sabotaging his master — better, but it left his speech empty.
  • Afraid of being replaced — adopted, then rejected in revision. It depended on the marked character being what his master needed; under the Gathering, they are not. And a Ventrue still jockeying for precedence is too much person for a mind fully overwritten.
  • Adopted: a broken shell. Fully dominated since 1905. He fights as a guard dog because he is one. The last of him is spent in the lucid moment, begging the coterie to reseal the tomb and pointing them to his research.

The confrontation happens at the cellar threshold, not in the tomb. Ashcroft loses, and the Architect takes what is left of him to tell the coterie, honestly, to go home. This gives the whole group their one direct encounter with the antagonist, while reserving the name and the offer for the sarcophagus.

E.6 — The Marked Character

Rejected: the keystone component. See E.2, Version 3. Adopted: the one who gets the offer. At the sarcophagus the Architect speaks into that character's mind alone — honest, sincerely believed, and including his name. The rest of the table sees their friend standing motionless and cannot tell refusal from consideration. Fully secret; the character need never report it.

Adopted: the table knows there is a marked player, but not what it means or how much is being shared. Two channels — open makes the group afraid for them, closed makes the group uncertain about them.

Adopted: early, not chosen. Unbound at the tomb only because the coterie moved fast.

Adopted: both sides need them. The reseal needs the blood of the line; the Architect needs them to do nothing. The choice Kanefer refused, handed to a neonate.

Adopted: the marked character as a clue conduit. The coterie comes to rely on information that exists only because their friend is being consumed. This is the personal-horror weight the original draft lacked, and it belongs to the whole table.

E.7 — The Chant

This went through two models.

Rejected: strict selectivity — only the King's line affected, nothing for anyone else. It preserved the political signal but left three of four players with no personal stake.

Adopted: two effects.

  • The Dread — universal. The Chant speaks to the Beast, not the mind. Frenzy checks with no trigger. Scales upward with generation: elders devastated, thin-bloods nearly untouched — for whom it has become a banner.
  • The Binding — King's line only, hidden underneath the Dread, presenting differently in every case.

Adopted in revision: the Chant compounds. Every bound will strengthens it. The chronicle begins at the crescendo, which explains why fifty years of Chant produced a crisis only in May 1998.

Why the two-effect model is better: the Dread is camouflage. It is loud enough to hide the signal, which is why a Court of paranoid centenarians hasn't seen a single-clan pattern. And the discovery becomes something the players earn rather than something they are told. No NPC reaches it first.

E.8 — Gideon Oakes and the Native American Material

Considered and deliberately kept: the mystical guide archetype. The Storyteller's position: the contrast between a folk-memory guardian and the World of Darkness is the point, and it is what makes Gideon work. This was a conscious choice, not an oversight.

Adopted: Mohican, not Seneca — historically correct for Greene County. The Mohicans were allied to the Crown in 1758, which makes the attack on a British company inexplicable in the records. Kept deliberately: only the oath explains it.

Problem solved: why he is alive. The Pact forbids the thing it guards from harming threshold-blood. Ashcroft's clinic chalkboard is a record of repeatedly failing to get permission to kill an old farmer. Adopted: the Pact now works against its maker. His ancestors swore to defend Kanefer's sleep. Kanefer wants to wake. The oath-keepers are still doing exactly what their lord asked four thousand years ago, and it puts them against him. Problem solved: what if the players kill him. The Pact protects him from the Architect's side of the board only. If the coterie kills him, the ward does not fail: his map and the marked character's growing sight get them through instead. No dead end.

E.9 — Structure and Setting

Adopted: the open city. After Cold Storage, Chapter One is a web of nineteen real New York locations joined by leads and doors, with sparse clues, a gated finale at City Hall station, the Gravediggers as the physical third party, the Society of Leopold only as whispers, and a Court that collapses in five stages.

Adopted: the opening night. The coterie is at their mezzanine table at Cold Storage when the Wakeners take the suspended DJ booth, kill the DJ, and sing. A Courage roll decides each character's night: frozen by the Dread, or an aimed frenzy at the Singers, with Willpower buying single controlled turns either way. The Singers are martyrs — they never run, and the Chant ends only when the last of them dies. The elders get there first, and their straight line to the booth runs through the crowd. The coterie is handed the case by an exhausted Sheriff who has already decided it was a blood frenzy. The rumour banks moved from the Introduction into §2.8, with Cold Storage as the first chance to use them.

Adopted: six chapters. The Lodge and the tomb were split into separate chapters (Four and Five), and the offer, the reseal and the Gathering became Chapter Six, Blood at the Threshold.

Adopted: Chapters One and Two are deliberately broad. New York is where the coterie spends substantial time assembling clues — dead stations, mad scrawl, zealots. The road north will be fleshed out with more incident. Broken Arrow was simply the most developed chapter in the original draft, not the intended weighting.

Adopted: the Prince stays out of it. No secret agenda. Wild rumours only. The coterie is tasked by the Sheriff.

Adopted: late-1990s NYC as rotten, not cleaned up. The Giuliani surface is a thin bright veneer over a city that files every vampiric act under crime and is surprised by none of it. The subway after midnight is a calculation.

Adopted names: Greywick Lodge. Broken Arrow Ridge in every pre-1890s document, Sawtooth Ridge on every modern map — renamed by Ashcroft through the county surveyor to bury the connection. E.10 — Standing Principles

The rules that emerged from all of the above, and that every future section should obey:

  • Every mechanism should be explainable in one sentence. When it takes more, it is wrong.
  • Nobody in this story explains it. The coterie assembles it from objects, rooms, and behaviour.
  • One antagonist with intent. Everything else is consequence.
  • Honesty is scarier than deceit. The Architect never lies.
  • Every tier is someone's instrument. King over Architect, Architect over Ashcroft, Ashcroft over the Wakeners, the Wakeners over the city — and at the bottom, one player character, and the question of whether their friends will do to them what has been done to everyone else.
  • Never let one NPC be the only door.
  • Moral cost should never be a puzzle with a correct answer.

E.11 — The Tomb and the Taking

Rejected: domination on touching the basalt. It could not explain why pickaxes and Ashcroft's own Embrace on the stone in 1758 disturbed nothing.

Adopted: the basalt is the roof; the sarcophagus is the door. Ashcroft broke into the outer tomb in 1893 and spent twelve years mapping a maze and translating its walls before touching the sarcophagus in 1905. That window holds the title handout, gives the coterie maps to follow in Chapter Five, and puts the reseal and the offer in the same room.

Adopted: the reseal lives in Ashcroft's research — as a footnote at the bottom of a pile, copied down around 1901 and never thought about again. Not hidden; simply never needed. He remembers it's there. The rite needs the blood of the King's line. The Architect has never read the notes.

Fixed: the recordings. Wax cylinder (1898), wire (1940), cassette (1995). Cassette tape did not exist before the 1960s.
